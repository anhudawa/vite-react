There is no purer expression of what this whole site is about than the four-minute mile, and
it took me a while to work out why. It is not the fastest event, or the longest, or the
hardest in any simple sense. It is the one whose entire meaning sits on a watch. Strip the
timing away and a mile is just four laps of a man running quite fast. Put the clock back, and
draw a single line across it at four minutes, and you have one of the most freighted numbers
in the history of sport.

For years that number was treated as a wall. Doctors and coaches half-believed the body could
not be driven a mile inside four minutes — that something would give first, that it was a
physiological edge rather than a figure on a dial. The barrier was real to people in exactly
the way a watch makes things real: a precise threshold, indifferent and unarguable, that you
were either on the right side of or you were not.

## A number, then a time

On 6 May 1954, at the Iffley Road track in Oxford, Roger Bannister ran the mile in three
minutes and 59.4 seconds. He was a medical student who trained on his lunch breaks. Chris
Brasher set the early pace and Chris Chataway carried it on, and Bannister came off the
last bend into a headwind that had nearly made him call the attempt off, and held the line
when everything in him wanted to break it. The barrier that was supposed to be physical turned
out to be arithmetic. He was the first man past it. John Landy was the second, a few weeks
later, which is usually how walls work once someone shows you they were only ever a number.

The detail I love, the one that gives the whole thing away, is the announcement. Norris
McWhirter read the result out to the crowd and drew it into theatre on purpose. "The time was
three…" — and the rest was lost, because three was all anyone needed. The four had been
beaten. The decimal, the 59.4, was a formality. A stadium full of people lost its mind over a
single digit on a stopwatch, because that digit was the entire point. That is what it looks
like when a sport is, at its root, an argument with a clock.

## Hand-held, and that mattered

It was caught on stopwatches, held by officials at the line, thumbs on the buttons. There is
something fitting in that — the record that mattered most to the century was taken by the
plainest possible instrument, a hand and a watch and a held breath, the same tool a club
timekeeper holds at a Tuesday-night track meet now. The chronograph on a collector's wrist and
the official's stopwatch at Iffley Road are the same idea wearing different clothes: a device
that exists to start, hold, and report an interval, because someone needs to know exactly how
long a hard thing took.

The mile makes the case for the whole category better than any luxury catalogue could. A
watch's deepest job was never decoration. It was to be the honest witness at the finish line,
the thing that tells you whether the suffering came in under the number or just over it. For
most of us, most days, that distinction is private and small. For ninety seconds on a spring
evening in 1954 it was the most-watched number in the world.

## The long second, made literal

This is why distance running sits at the centre of this site rather than at its edge. The mile,
the 5,000, the 10,000, the steeplechase, the marathon — these are the events where the clock
is not a sponsor's logo or a piece of timing infrastructure but the actual opponent. You do
not beat another runner so much as you beat a time, and the other runners are simply other
people trying to beat the same one. The watch is the adversary, the judge, and the only thing
in the stadium that cannot be argued with.

Bannister chased a number and changed it, and in doing so he proved a thing this brand keeps
trying to say in smaller ways: that the most serious relationship in sport is the one between
an athlete and a clock. Not the watch as jewellery. The watch as the line you are running at,
the figure you are trying to get under before your body decides the matter for you. Three
minutes, fifty-nine point four. It is still, seventy years on, the most famous time in the
history of running, and it is a watch reading, nothing more and nothing less.
