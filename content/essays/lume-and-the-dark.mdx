At hour four of a night run, you look down and the dial gives you almost nothing. A grey-green suggestion of hands. You tilt the wrist, cup a palm around it, and read the time the way you read trail in fog — by inference. That moment has a century of history behind it, and knowing the history changes how you plan for it.

## The price of the first glow

The first luminous dials were painted with radium. United States Radium sold the paint under the brand name Undark, and from around 1917 its factory in Orange, New Jersey employed young women to apply it to watch and instrument dials. They were taught to point the brush between their lips — lip, dip, paint — hundreds of times a day, and they were told the paint was harmless. It was not. The women began to sicken: anaemia, fractures, and a necrosis of the jaw now remembered as radium jaw. Five of them, led by Grace Fryer, sued their former employer; it had taken Fryer two years just to find a lawyer willing to take the case. They settled in 1928, each receiving $10,000 and a $600-a-year annuity, most of them already dying. Their cases helped push occupational disease law into being. Every glowing dial since owes them something.

The physics adds a cold footnote. Radium-226 has a half-life of roughly 1,600 years. When a vintage radium dial goes dark, it is because decades of radiation broke down the zinc sulfide phosphor — the radium itself has barely begun to decay. The light dies. The hazard stays.

## Twelve years to half

Through the 1960s the industry moved to tritium paint. Tritium's beta radiation is feeble — it cannot penetrate the watch glass, or even the outer layer of skin — and Swiss rules from the mid-1960s required a T on the dial to declare it: T SWISS MADE T. The trade-off runs the other way from radium. Tritium's half-life is about 12.3 years, so the glow itself is mortal. A tritium dial from the 1980s is safe on the wrist and dark in the drawer, its light simply spent.

## Light you carry in

Almost everything sold today wears Super-LumiNova or something like it: strontium aluminate doped with europium and dysprosium, invented at the Japanese firm Nemoto in 1993 and, since a 1998 joint venture with the Swiss company RC Tritec, produced under the Super-LumiNova name. It is not radioactive at all. It behaves like a rechargeable battery for light — it absorbs photons from sunlight or a lamp, then returns them, up to ten times brighter than the old zinc sulfide paints.

The catch is the curve. Photoluminescent lume gives back most of its stored energy early. It blazes in the first minutes, dims hard over the first hours, then rides a long faint tail toward morning.

## Light that burns alone

The alternative is the tritium tube: a sealed glass capillary lined with phosphor and filled with tritium gas, the kind mb-microtec builds as trigalight for its own brand Traser and for names like Ball. Nothing to charge. The glow is constant all night, every night; Ball markets its tubes as glowing for up to 25 years. The decay still applies — after two half-lives, call it 25 years, a tube emits roughly a quarter of its original light — but within a tube's working life the difference between midnight and 4 a.m. is nothing at all.

## Late in the night

So what should you expect? Charge deliberately: a few minutes of direct sunlight or a bright lamp on the dial before the start, not ambient kitchen light. Expect the fade. Deep into the night, Super-LumiNova is typically a whisper — readable in true darkness, to dark-adapted eyes, and easily washed out by moonlight on snow or a passing car. The higher grades, A and X1, are formulated to hold their brightness longer into the night, and green C3 tends to read brightest to the human eye.

If you run with a headlamp, none of this matters much: every sweep of the beam across your wrist tops the lume back up, and a few deliberate seconds of direct light will typically buy you minutes of easy reading. If your nights are long and your light discipline strict — alpine starts, moonlight rides, races where the lamp stays off — tubes are the answer. They give less than a freshly charged dial and far more than one that has been fading since the start, and they ask nothing from you while you work.
