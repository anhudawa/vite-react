On the night of 22 October 1707, a Royal Navy fleet came home from the Mediterranean in dirty weather, twenty-one ships under Admiral Sir Cloudesley Shovell running for the Channel. The navigators believed they were in safe water. Legend has a council of sailing masters fixing the fleet near Ushant, off Brittany; the surviving logs record no such meeting, only the mistake itself. The ships were bearing down on the Isles of Scilly. Four struck the rocks. Somewhere between 1,400 and 2,000 men drowned on their own coast.

They did not die for want of seamanship. They died for want of a number.

## Time is a place

Latitude, a sailor could take from the sky — the height of the noon sun, a quadrant, printed tables. Longitude gave nothing away. The Earth turns fifteen degrees an hour, which makes east–west position a clock problem: carry the time of your home port with you, note the moment of your local noon, and the difference between the two is where you stand. It also makes the cost of error easy to count. Two minutes of clock error is half a degree of longitude — about thirty nautical miles at the equator. Enough to put a fleet on the rocks.

The catch was the carrying. No clock then built could keep time at sea. Pendulums are useless on a rolling deck; heat and cold alter metal and oil. Isaac Newton, advising a Commons committee in 1714, allowed that a watch could do it — "but, by reason of the motion of the Ship, the Variation of Heat and Cold, Wet and Dry ... such a watch hath not yet been made." Learned opinion mostly looked to the moon and stars instead.

That same year, petitioned by merchants and sea captains, Parliament passed the Longitude Act — royal assent on 9 July 1714 — offering £10,000 for a method good to one degree, £15,000 to forty minutes of arc, £20,000 to half a degree. The Scilly dead are usually named as the reason; the paper trail is thinner than the legend, but the fear was real, and general. In today's money, the top prize runs to millions.

## The joiner from Foulby

John Harrison was born in Yorkshire in 1693, a carpenter by trade and a clockmaker by self-instruction; his earliest clocks were built almost entirely of wood. His first sea clock, H1, was finished in 1735 and trialled on a voyage to Lisbon the following year, where Harrison corrected the ship's landfall — the Lizard, not the Start, sixty miles of difference. Then came the long middle of his life. H2 never went to sea. H3 took nearly two decades and never satisfied him.

What happened next is the part worth sitting with. Harrison abandoned the sea clock as a species. The answer, he decided, was a watch. H4, completed in 1759, is about thirteen centimetres across and beats five times a second, its [quick, heavy balance](/features/the-escapement-the-part-that-lets-go) riding out motion that would have wrecked a pendulum. It looks like an overgrown pocket watch. It is arguably the most consequential watch ever made.

## Two months to Jamaica

On 18 November 1761, Harrison's son William sailed from Portsmouth aboard HMS Deptford, H4 in his keeping. They reached Jamaica on 19 January 1762 and checked the watch, corrected for its known daily rate, against the longitude of the port.

<Pull cite="H4, JAMAICA, 1762">Five seconds slow, two months out from Portsmouth. Call it a nautical mile of ocean.</Pull>

The Board of Longitude did not simply pay. It demanded a second trial — Barbados, 1764, where H4 again performed within the Act's strictest limit — then released the money in halves, hedged with conditions. Harrison received a final £8,750 from Parliament in 1773, aged eighty, after George III took up his cause; the king had tested Harrison's later H5 himself and found it good to a third of a second a day. Harrison died in 1776.

## Sailing against time

Satellites ended the chronometer's working life; position is now a free good, accurate to metres. But the contest H4 was built to survive still leaves from Les Sables-d'Olonne every four years. The Vendée Globe sends sailors around the planet alone, non-stop and unassisted, and the 2024–25 edition was won in 64 days and 19 hours, the fastest in the race's history. Nobody aboard is navigating by a spring anymore.

Chronometer, before it was [a word on a dial](/guides/what-a-chronometer-actually-is), named a machine you could bet a ship on. Larcum Kendall's copy of H4, called K1, went around the world with Captain Cook — the bet taken again, and won again.
