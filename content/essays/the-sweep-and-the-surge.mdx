The seconds hand on a good mechanical watch does not tick. It sweeps — eight, sometimes
ten small steps a second, fast enough to read as continuous motion. People think the
sweep is a luxury. It is closer to an honesty. The hand is showing you, in real time,
that the escapement underneath is beating cleanly, evenly, many times a second, and has
not faltered.

A jerk in the sweep is a fault you can see from across a room. Everything else about the
movement is hidden behind the caseback. The seconds hand is the one part that performs
its regulation in public.

## The rider's seconds hand

Watch a time trial in the last kilometre, when the rider is empty and the only thing
left is form. The good ones do not surge. The power line on the data afterwards is
almost flat — a hand sweeping, not ticking. They are spending the last of the reserve
at exactly the rate that empties the tank on the line and not a metre before.

It looks like nothing is happening. That is the tell. The enormous effort of holding a
steady release, when every nerve is asking you to either give up or empty everything at
once, reads from the outside as serene. The crowd cheers the rider who attacks and
blows up. The connoisseur watches the one whose seconds hand never stuttered.

<Pull cite="On craft">
  The hardest thing a movement does, and the hardest thing an athlete does, is to look
  like it is doing nothing at all.
</Pull>

## Craft is the part you do not see failing

This is the quiet thesis under everything we cover. The price of a watch is visible.
The result of a race is visible. The craft in both — the even release, the regulation
held under load — is nearly invisible, and it is the only part that was ever hard.

We write about the part you cannot see. The sweep, not the dial. The pacing, not the
podium. It is a smaller audience that cares about this. It is also the only audience
worth writing for.
