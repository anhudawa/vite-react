The sprinter comes off the back on the first climb, and the race lets him go. Nothing dramatic happens. The road tilts, the pace at the front holds, a gap opens that will not close today. There are 140 kilometres left, three more mountains, and from here his day is governed by a number he cannot yet know.

That number is the time cut.

## A deadline still being written

Grand Tour stages carry an elimination time. Finish outside it and you are out — not of the stage, of the race. The limit is a percentage of the winner's finishing time, and the percentage flexes with the day: each stage is assigned a difficulty coefficient, and the allowance grows with that coefficient and with the winner's average speed. Harder stage, more margin. Faster race, more margin again. On serious mountain days the window can run well past ten per cent of the winner's time.

But the winner has not finished. The riders at the back are pacing themselves against a deadline still being written, forty minutes up the road, by men racing for entirely different reasons. Their target is a fraction of an unknown.

So the dropped riders do the only sensible thing. They form a bus.

## The autobus

The French call it the autobus; the Italians, gruppetto — the little group, though on the worst days it is anything but little. Sprinters, spent domestiques, the sick and the crashed, gathered into a rolling truce between rival teams. Riders who would not concede a wheel in a finale hand each other food and pace each other up cols. The old nickname is the laughing group — a name for the mood that hides how exact the work is.

Every bus has a driver — an experienced rider who runs the numbers. Eros Poli did the job in the nineties; Bernhard Eisel, a veteran of nineteen Grand Tours, made a late career of it, studying the roadbook the night before to map where the minutes would be lost and where they could be bought back. The craft is simple to state, brutal to execute: climb at the slowest pace that still adds up, descend as if the result depends on it, and form a paceline through the valleys to recover what the mountains took. All day the bus rides not against the riders ahead but against an estimate of them. "You don't know their time exactly," Matteo Trentin said after one close call, "so you have to calculate with experience."

Anyone who has [cracked in a long event](/features/seventeen-hours) knows how hard that is. Dropped, you want either to chase — fatal — or to drift — also fatal. The autobus holds the line between, hour after hour, on legs that have already failed once today.

The trap is the short mountain stage. Less road means a smaller winning time, and a smaller winning time means a smaller allowance — a percentage of not very much. At the 2016 Giro, on a 132-kilometre stage to Andalo, the gruppetto raced flat out from the gun and still cut it fine. Trentin's close call was that day: inside the limit, he reckoned, "by a hair."

## The safety valve

The rulebook keeps an escape hatch. Commissaires can extend the limit in exceptional circumstances, and when eliminations would run past roughly a fifth of the field, the cut can be stretched to keep the race whole. Mercy has a price: reinstated riders are docked points. On the 2011 Tour stage that finished atop the Galibier, 88 riders — more than half of what remained of the race — came home outside the limit. The jury let them stay and took 20 points from each man, a sanction that lands hardest on the sprinters, whose green-jersey hopes are counted in that currency; Mark Cavendish, then leading that competition, paid it with the rest. The next day, on Alpe d'Huez, more than half the field needed the same reprieve. They had climbed all day to stay in a race they could not win, and paid the fine anyway.

## Reading the back of the race

Television lives at the front. The better lesson is often forty minutes behind it, where the sport's fastest finishers become its most careful timekeepers. The stage winner throws his arms up; the bus is still out there, checking the gap, dividing the remaining road into climbs to survive and descents to spend. Its result is binary — inside or out — and it rides five or six hours to land one number on the right side of another.

It is timekeeping as a team sport. No jersey records it. Nothing on the results sheet distinguishes a bus driven to within ninety seconds of elimination from an easy day in the saddle. But tomorrow the sprinter starts, and on the flat run to the line he is once again the fastest man in the race — because the day before, out of the cameras' reach, the bus did the maths together and got it right.
