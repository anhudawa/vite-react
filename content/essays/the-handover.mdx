A race ends twice. The first finish is the official one, at the line, when the chip crosses the mat and the clock stops being yours. The second happens later, in the car park or the kitchen, and nobody records it: the GPS watch comes off with the race number, and a slower watch goes on in its place. This piece is about the second finish — the handover — and why it may be the part of the two-watch practice most worth keeping.

## The morning half

The ritual runs in both directions, and it starts before dawn. Race morning has a dress code. The GPS goes on before the coffee is finished — charged overnight, satellites acquired in the car park, the strap threaded one hole tighter than usual because you will want it snug over the wrist bone at kilometre thirty. The mechanical stays behind on the nightstand, running through everything you are about to do to yourself.

Putting it down is part of getting dressed for the work. The wrist you take to the start line carries nothing older than this season: a battery, a satellite fix, a lap button under a cold thumb. Whatever the day turns out to be, it will arrive home as data.

## The transfer

Hours later the order reverses, and this is the scene the whole practice is built around. The chute empties you out into ordinary space, and you begin taking the racer apart. The number comes unpinned. The timing chip comes off the shoe. The GPS comes off in the same series of gestures because it belongs to the same costume, and the three of them go into the kit bag together. The file uploads. The effort is a record now, sealed, arguable with no one.

Then the other buckle.

The watch from the nightstand — or from the bag's side pocket, wrapped in a sock, if the race was far from home — has been running the whole time, indifferent to your splits. Fastening it takes ten seconds of fumbling with stiff fingers, and it changes the day more than the medal does. Racer's time is over. The dinner, the drive home, the Monday when the stairs must be taken one at a time: all of that belongs to the slower watch.

The line on the road is where the effort stops being measured. The wrist is where it stops being the point. The real boundary between the two lives was never painted on the tarmac; it is the moment of transfer, and you perform it yourself, alone, with no one timing you at all.

## Both wrists at once

There is a way to skip the ritual, and it has royal precedent. The Prince of Wales has been photographed wearing the Omega Seamaster his mother gave him on one wrist and a Garmin Forerunner on the other — sentiment on one arm, cadence on the other. Nothing wrong with it, and on a travel day it is even sensible. But the stack answers a different question. Worn together, the two watches say what you own. Worn in sequence, they say where you are: still inside the effort, or after it. A boundary you never cross stops being a boundary.

## What the handover is for

It would be easy to mistake the ritual for caution, as if the mechanical comes off so the racing cannot break it. The engineering says otherwise. When IWC's research division measured what sport actually does to a wristwatch, the loads came in at roughly 20 to 60G, and the division's head, Lorenz Brunner, says a 60G golf swing gives a mechanical watch — at least one of IWC's — absolutely no problem. You could very likely run the whole marathon in the mechanical and hand it on to the next generation afterwards, unbothered.

So the handover is not protection, and it is not a verdict on which watch is better at which job either — that case is [the argument](/guides/gps-watch-vs-mechanical), made in full elsewhere on this site. This is the practice that follows from it. Once each watch has its hours, someone has to change the shifts, and the changing turns out to carry a meaning neither watch holds alone.

Endurance sport is good at beginnings and bad at endings, which tend to dribble away in foil blankets and recovery shakes. The handover gives the ending a shape. One buckle closes on everything the morning demanded, and the season's most accurate watch goes into a drawer to wait for the next number. The other watch takes up the long shift it was made for: everything the effort is for, which turns out to be most of a life.
