The convention is every four to seven years. It is a convention, not a law, and the manufacturers themselves have been quietly stretching it: Rolex now recommends a service approximately every ten years depending on model and use, and Omega suggests a complete service every five to eight, with a water-resistance check annually. If your watch keeps time the way it always has, winds smoothly, and stays dry inside, you have latitude. If any of those has changed, the interval is over, whatever the calendar says.

That is the answer. The rest is why, and what happens when you finally hand the watch over.

## Where the interval comes from

A mechanical movement runs on oil — tiny, precisely placed amounts of it, at the pivots and jewels where metal turns against stone thousands of times an hour. Those lubricants age. They migrate, dry, and collect whatever dust finds its way past the gaskets. The failure is quiet: friction rises, the balance swings through shorter arcs, timekeeping starts to wander. By the time a watch stops outright, wear that a routine service would have prevented may already be cutting into steel.

Modern synthetic oils last far longer than what watchmakers used fifty years ago, which is why published intervals keep creeping outward. Treat any brand's figure as specific to that brand's movements and warranties. Treat four to seven years as the general shape of the thing.

Gaskets are the exception to all this patience. Rubber ages on the calendar whether you wear the watch or not. If your watch swims with you — and around here it probably does — have the seals pressure-tested yearly. It is quick, it is cheap, and it is the difference between a service and a rescue.

## What a service actually is

A full service is closer to a rebuild than an oil change. The movement comes out of the case and comes fully apart — every wheel, spring, and bridge. The parts go through ultrasonic cleaning to strip old lubricant and debris. The watchmaker inspects each pivot and jewel under magnification, replaces what is worn — mainsprings commonly, gaskets always — then reassembles the movement with fresh oils at the correct points, in the correct amounts. The watch is regulated on a timing machine, cased up, pressure-tested, and observed for days to confirm it holds its rate in different positions.

Expect the whole thing to take weeks, sometimes months at a brand service center. The bench time is days; the queue is the rest.

## What it costs

Prices vary enough by region, movement, and condition that anyone quoting you a single number is guessing. In the United States, a time-only automatic on a common movement — an ETA, a Sellita, a Seiko — commonly lands somewhere between $150 and $350 at a good independent watchmaker; co-axial and in-house calibers run higher, into the $400s and beyond. Manufacturer service centers generally charge more — often several hundred to well over a thousand dollars — and complications push higher from there. Chronographs cost more than three-handers. Vintage costs more than modern, because parts must be sourced or made.

A fair watchmaker will inspect the watch and give you an estimate before committing you to anything. Ask for it. And amortize before you flinch: five hundred dollars across seven years is modest against what most of us spend on chains and cassettes in a single season.

## Signs it is overdue

- **Timekeeping drift.** A watch that held five seconds a day and now wanders twenty has changed for a reason.
- **Short power reserve.** Fully wound, it stops hours before it should. Either the mainspring has tired or the friction it fights has grown.
- **A gritty crown.** Winding should feel smooth and slightly alive. Grinding or stiffness means wear or dirt where neither belongs.
- **Noise.** A rotor that rattles or scrapes is asking for attention.
- **Fog under the crystal.** An emergency, not a schedule item. Moisture corrodes fast. Go now.

One caveat before you book anything: a watch that suddenly runs fast, minutes a day, is often magnetized rather than worn out. Demagnetizing takes a watchmaker seconds, often while you wait. Rule it out first.

## The rider's version

You already run this discipline on your bike. You replace chains by wear, not superstition, and you do not wait for the bottom bracket to announce itself on a climb. The watch asks for the same posture. Test the seals yearly if it gets wet. Service on evidence when the watch offers any, and on the calendar when it has been long enough that you are running on luck. Keep the paperwork. And ask for your replaced parts back — with vintage pieces especially, what came out of the case is part of what you own.
