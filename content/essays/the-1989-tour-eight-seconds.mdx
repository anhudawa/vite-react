The whole argument this site keeps trying to make — that the deepest relationship in sport is
the one between an athlete and a clock — was settled once and for all on a Sunday in July 1989,
and nobody has ever had to make it again. The 1989 Tour de France ran for eighty-seven hours of
racing across 3,285 kilometres. It came down to eight seconds. There is no rounding that into a
metaphor. It is just the number, and it is the smallest margin the race has ever been won by.

Greg LeMond went into the final stage fifty seconds behind Laurent Fignon. Fifty seconds, in a
Grand Tour, is a wall. It is the kind of lead you do not lose on the last day, which is why the
last day had so rarely been allowed to matter — the Tour usually arrives in Paris already
decided, the final ride a procession. But 1989 ended on a 24.5-kilometre individual time trial
into the city, [a man alone against the clock](/features/the-longest-hour), which is the one format where fifty seconds is
not quite safe.

## The ride

LeMond rode it on aero bars, head down, in a position the sport had barely seen, and averaged
54.545 kilometres an hour — the fastest individual time trial anyone had ridden in the history
of the Tour to that point. Fignon rode it the old way, upright, and crossed the line, and then
had to stand there while the clock finished its sentence. He had lost the Tour de France, after
three weeks and more than three thousand kilometres, by eight seconds.

Sit with how thin that is. Eight seconds is a held breath. It is less than the gap between two
sprinters you would call a dead heat by eye. It is the time it takes to read this sentence. And
it was spread, invisibly, across twenty-three days of racing, accumulating a fraction here and
a fraction there, until it sat there at the finish as the entire difference between winning the
biggest race in the sport and not.

## Why the watch had to be right

This is where the timekeeping stops being trivia and becomes the point. Tissot was the [official
timekeeper](/dispatch/omega-and-the-olympic-clock) of the Tour de France from 1988 to 1992, which means it was the Tissot clock that
counted Fignon down and found him eight seconds short. A margin like that is only a fact if the
instrument measuring it is beyond question. If the timing is loose — if it is good to a second
either way — then eight seconds is not a result, it is an argument, and the closest Tour in
history dissolves into a dispute nobody could ever settle.

It did not dissolve, because the watch was better than the doubt. That is the unglamorous,
load-bearing role timing plays in endurance sport, and it is the reason a watch company belongs
at the finish line rather than just on the wrist. Pacing, suffering, the years of training — all
of it pours, in the end, into a number, and the number only means something if something honest
is there to read it. In 1989 the something was a Tissot clock, and it held an eight-second truth
steady while two careers hung off it.

## The long second, at the highest level

I have ridden [time trials, the small local kind](/features/casio-f-91w-and-the-time-trial), and lost them by a handful of seconds, and I
can tell you that nothing teaches you what a second is worth like watching one tick past after
you have given everything you own. Multiply that feeling by the Tour de France and you have
Fignon at the line in Paris, a great rider who did almost everything right across three weeks
and was beaten by an interval most of life is too coarse to notice.

That is the long second made literal, at the very top of the sport. Not the watch as jewellery,
not the watch as a sponsor's logo, but the watch as the final judge — the only object at the
finish line honest and precise enough to separate the closest two riders the race has ever
produced. Eight seconds. The whole 1989 Tour, and a fair part of this brand's reason for
existing, lives inside them.
