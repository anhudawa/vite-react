On 29 May 1953, Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay stood on the summit of Everest, the
first confirmed ascent in the mountain's history. They had a few minutes up there before
the descent demanded all their attention again, and the weather and the thin air left
little room for ceremony. What happened on that summit is, in the parts that matter,
settled. Two men reached the highest point on earth and came back down to tell it. The
rest of the history is harder going.

Because somewhere between that morning and now, a smaller story attached itself to the
big one, and it concerns a watch. The popular version is easy to recite: a Rolex was on
the wrist that reached the top, the first watch to the roof of the world, proof of a
movement that could keep time where almost nothing else could keep working. It is a
lovely line. It has been told so often, in so many catalogues and captions, that it now
sits in most people's minds as plain fact. It is not quite that.

## What the record actually carries

Rolex was associated with the 1953 expedition, and its watches were used on it. That much
is real, and worth saying clearly before anything else, because the firm did supply timepieces
to the climb and there is nothing invented about that connection. The leap that the
popular story makes is from *present on the expedition* to *first to the very top*, and
that leap is mostly the work of marketing rather than the record.

The better-supported account points elsewhere. A British-made Smiths watch is generally
credited with having reached the summit, and Smiths said as much afterwards, publicising
the achievement in the years that followed. So the two firms ended up telling overlapping
stories about the same mountain on the same day, and over time the louder, better-funded
one won the room. The watch most people now picture on that summit is, on the evidence,
probably not the one the evidence best supports.

I want to be careful here, because being careful is the whole point. I cannot tell you the
exact reference that went highest, or whose wrist carried which watch through the final
hours, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Those specifics are contested. The honest
position is that a Smiths is the watch with the stronger claim to the summit, that Rolex's
summit claim is an association the firm built into something grander than the record
warrants, and that the precise inventory of the last few hundred metres is not fully
settled. Anyone who hands you a cleaner story than that is selling something, possibly a
watch.

## Why brands tell it this way

It would be easy to be sour about this, and I would rather not be. The pull toward the
Everest story is understandable, and from a certain angle it is even sympathetic. A watch
firm makes an instrument meant to keep working when conditions turn against it. Here is the
hardest place on the planet, the thinnest air, the deepest cold, and here is a real
expedition that really used the firm's watches. Of course you want to plant a flag on that.
The summit is the most legible [proof of toughness](/features/the-overbuilt-watch) anyone could ask for, and the temptation
to round *we were there* up to *we were first to the top* is enormous.

Heritage is genuinely valuable, and a brand that has been making serious instruments for a
long time has earned the right to talk about it. The trouble is only that [heritage marketing](/features/the-model-from-feat-lineage)
tends to sand the corners off the record until the story is smoother than the truth. A caveat
gets dropped. A *probably* hardens into a *did*. A watch that was on the expedition becomes
the watch on the summit. None of it requires a lie, exactly. It requires a hundred small
roundings-up, each forgivable, that add up to a history that did not happen.

## Getting it right as the product

This is where I have to declare an interest, because The Long Second is a brand built on
mechanical watches and on accuracy, and accuracy is not a slogan we get to keep if we only
apply it to [seconds-per-day](/guides/what-a-chronometer-actually-is). The same discipline that times a movement honestly has to time
a story honestly. If we will repeat any flattering tale that moves a watch off the shelf,
then the precision we claim is decorative, and a reader is right to trust none of it.

So the correction is not a gotcha, and it is not a swipe at Rolex, a firm that makes
extraordinary things and does not need my defending or my scolding. The correction is the
work. Saying *the Smiths has the stronger claim, the Rolex summit story is mostly marketing,
and the fine detail of that final climb is genuinely uncertain* is a more useful sentence
than the myth, precisely because it tells you where the solid ground ends. You can stand on
it. You always know, with an honest account, exactly how much weight it will bear.

The mountain does not care which firm gets the credit. Hillary and Tenzing reached the top
on a May morning in 1953, and that is the fact the watches were lucky enough to be near. The
least we owe the moment is to describe it at the resolution the record can actually support,
and to admit, out loud, the places where even that runs out.
