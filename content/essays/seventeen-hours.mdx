It started as an argument that needed settling. At an awards do on Oahu in 1977, a Navy officer
named John Collins and his wife Judy fell to wondering which athletes were really the fittest —
the swimmers, the cyclists, or the runners — and decided the only honest way to find out was to
make them do all three in a single day. They took three races that already existed on the island,
the 2.4-mile Waikiki Roughwater Swim, the 112-mile bike around the island, and the 26.2-mile
Honolulu Marathon, and bolted them end to end. On the entry form Collins wrote the line that
would outlive everything else about the day: *Swim 2.4 miles! Bike 112 miles! Run 26.2 miles!
Brag for the rest of your life.* Whoever came home first, he said, they'd call the Iron Man.

On 18 February 1978, fifteen men stood on the beach and set off to find out whether a body could
even do it. Twelve of them finished. The first was Gordon Haller, a Navy communications
specialist, in eleven hours, forty-six minutes and fifty-eight seconds — the first person on
earth entitled to the name. It was not a slick event. It was a dare with a stopwatch on it. But
inside that dare was a structure nobody had quite built before, and the structure is the reason
the Ironman became what it became, and the reason it belongs on a site about watches.

## The clock stops keeping score and starts hunting

In almost every race, the clock is a scorekeeper. It sits at the finish and records what you did,
and you are really racing the people around you. The Ironman, as it grew up, did something
stranger and more democratic: it set a hard limit. Most Ironman races today give you seventeen
hours from the morning gun, no more, and that single rule changes the entire nature of the clock.
It is no longer behind you, totting up your result. It is ahead of you, counting down, and for
all but the handful of professionals at the front, *that* is the opponent. Not the field. The
deadline.

This is the long second stretched to its absolute limit. Seventeen hours is not a sprint
finish or [an eight-second margin](/features/the-1989-tour-eight-seconds); it is most of a day and a night of continuous effort, and the
drama of it is wholly temporal. Somewhere out on the lava fields there is always a back-of-the-
field athlete doing [arithmetic in a failing body](/features/the-autobus) — *how far, how long, will the legs hold the
pace the clock demands* — and the finish-line clock ticking toward midnight is the most honest
and most pitiless judge in endurance sport. You can be magnificent and still lose to it. There
is an accidental poetry in the number, too: Collins himself, the man who dreamed the thing up,
came home that first day in just over seventeen hours. The founder's own long day sits, by pure
coincidence, right at the boundary the race would later draw.

## Why a watchmaker stands at this finish line

It is fitting that Breitling now keeps [the official time](/dispatch/omega-and-the-olympic-clock) at Ironman, because no event is more
literally *about* its clock. At the Tour de France the timing decides a winner; here the timing
decides whether thousands of ordinary, extraordinary people get to call themselves something for
the rest of their lives. The line between "Ironman" and "did not finish" is not a place on a road.
It is a moment on a watch. Cross before it and the word is yours forever; cross after and the same
distance, the same suffering, the same lava and salt and dark, earns nothing official at all. The
whole meaning of the day hangs on an instrument being exactly, unsentimentally right about when
midnight is.

That is the deepest version of this brand's one idea. The relationship between an athlete and a
clock is usually a quiet thing — a glance at the wrist, a split remembered. The Ironman makes it
loud and total. It takes Collins's daft, brilliant dare and hands it to anyone willing to spend a
day proving it on themselves, and then it does the cruel and generous thing of giving them a
deadline to do it inside. Seventeen hours. Brag for the rest of your life — but only if the watch
says you made it.
