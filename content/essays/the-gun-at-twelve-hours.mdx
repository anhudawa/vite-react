Twelve hours after the start, an official steps onto the finish line of the Comrades Marathon, raises a pistol, and fires. The shot is the cutoff. Runners still on the road — some of them close enough to read the gantry clock — are done. Seconds later a lone bugler plays The Last Post, and the better part of ninety kilometres of South African road between Durban and Pietermaritzburg empties for another year.

Most races let their deadlines dissolve. Timing mats get rolled up, volunteers drift off, the clock simply stops mattering. Comrades stages its deadline. There is a person, a firearm, a bugle. Time is made audible, and then it is made final.

## A Memorial That Moves

The race exists because of a war. Vic Clapham, a veteran of the First World War, conceived Comrades as a living memorial to soldiers who never came home, and the first edition went off on 24 May 1921. Thirty-four men started outside Pietermaritzburg City Hall. Sixteen made it to Durban inside the twelve-hour limit; Bill Rowan won in eight hours fifty-nine. The route alternates by year — the "up" run from Durban at sea level, the "down" run from Pietermaritzburg at 655 metres — and the exact distance shifts with the course, hovering near 88 kilometres.

Understand the origin and the finish-line theatre makes a different kind of sense. The Last Post is the call a military bugler plays over a grave. At Comrades it sounds every year, moments after the final gun, over the runners who did not arrive in time. The race was built as an act of remembrance, and the final gun is its liturgy. Clapham's memorial does not end with a shrug. It ends with a bugle.

## Eleven Hours, Then Twelve

The limit itself has history. Twelve hours was the allowance from 1921 to 1927; in 1928 it was cut to eleven, and eleven it stayed for three-quarters of a century. Only in 2003 did the race hand the twelfth hour back — and with it created a new medal, awarded for finishes between 11:00:00 and 11:59:59. They named it after Vic Clapham. The slowest official finish at the world's oldest ultramarathon carries the founder's name.

## The Deadline, Visible

I have run races where the cutoff was a rumour, a thing enforced somewhere behind you by a sweep vehicle you never saw. The Comrades cutoff happens in front of you, in a straight lined with grandstands, while you are sprinting for it. The final kilometre of a twelve-hour day is run at paces that should not be possible on those legs, because the deadline is visible and it is loud.

In 2017, Charlyn Smart covered the full course and was pulled aside at the line, roughly two minutes past the gun — the whole distance run, no finish recorded. In 2024, around 350 participants crossed after The Last Post had already played: they went the distance and got, in one observer's phrase, a technical knockout.

Comrades is decided on gun time — an era before chips and mats, preserved. A transponder may know precisely when you crossed, but the instant that governs the day is a hammer falling on a cartridge, the technology that has started footraces since the nineteenth century. Your wristwatch, dutifully counting up from the start, is advisory. The gun is law.

## What the Gun Keeps

The race keeps adjusting the frame around the ritual. This June, for the first time, Comrades started in three groups, each group's twelve hours timed from its own gun. And runners who missed the cutoff were no longer stopped at the line; they crossed, had their numbers scanned, and learned their status before the medal tent. The machinery softens. The principle does not: twelve hours, to the second, and a gun to say so.

The distance varies. The limit has moved. What the race has kept is the instant itself: a shot fired in front of the grandstands and a bugle answering it, with runners near enough to watch. Every other race lets you fail privately. Comrades makes the deadline a ceremony, because it was never only a deadline. It is the moment the memorial falls silent, and it has been kept, more or less faithfully, since 1921.
