This magazine spends most of its time inside the long second — the hour that refuses to pass on a climb, the watch ticking evenly while its wearer comes apart. Olympic sport runs on the other second, the compressed one, sliced into thousandths and examined frame by frame. We visit that world rarely, and this is a visit, not a move. But anyone who cares about timing owes something to the place where a second was first made to hold ten finishers at once.

## Thirty chronographs in a suitcase

The arrangement began modestly. For the 1932 Los Angeles Games, Omega became the first watchmaker to time an entire Olympics, and the operation consisted of one man traveling with 30 split-second chronographs. Split-second — rattrapante — because heats demanded two hands on one dial: stop one for the winner, let the other run. Those chronographs read to the tenth of a second, and behind each one stood a human thumb, with a human reaction time, deciding when the race had ended. The precision lived in the movement. The uncertainty lived in the person holding it.

That gap — between what the instrument could measure and what the hand could catch — is the story of the next forty years.

## The eye arrives

In 1948, at St. Moritz and then London, Omega brought the photoelectric cell to the finish line: a beam of light replaced the tape, and the crossing body broke it. London also put a slit photo-finish camera on an Olympic athletics track for the first time, recording the line continuously so that officials could read placings off film rather than memory.

The case for machines was made most brutally by their absence. At Rome in 1960, American Lance Larson and Australian John Devitt finished the 100-meter freestyle nearly together. The stopwatches favored Larson. The unofficial electronic backup favored Larson. The finish judges split, and the chief judge — who under the rules had no vote — gave Devitt the gold. The decision survived appeal. Larson kept the silver, his official time raised to 55.2 to match the winner's. Within a decade the sport had taken the call away from human eyes: at Mexico City in 1968, electronic timekeeping was used across every Olympic event for the first time, and swimmers stopped their own clocks by striking touchpads at the wall. The athlete's hand ended the race, not an official's opinion of it.

## The trouble with the thousandth

Then the instruments got ahead of the sport. Munich, 1972, the 400-meter individual medley: Sweden's Gunnar Larsson and America's Tim McKee both touched at 4:31.98.

Two thousandths of a second is a few millimeters of moving swimmer — finer, the sport concluded, than the tolerances to which a 50-meter pool is actually built. So the rules changed: results would be recorded to the hundredth, and identical times would stand as ties. Larsson kept his gold, McKee kept his silver, and a boundary was drawn around precision itself.

Athletics met the same wall in 2012, at the U.S. Olympic Trials, when Allyson Felix and Jeneba Tarmoh crossed the 100-meter line in 11.068 seconds each. Cameras running thousands of frames per second could not divide them, and no tiebreak procedure existed; the impasse ended only when Tarmoh ceded the Olympic spot. The finish line, examined closely enough, turned out to be a place where two people can stand at once.

## What we take back to the long second

Today Omega's Scan'O'Vision Ultimate photo-finish camera records up to 40,000 digital images per second, and its Quantum Timer resolves a millionth of a second, drifting no more than one second in ten million. By the company's own count it has held the Olympic timekeeping role on 31 occasions since that lone watchmaker opened his cases in Los Angeles.

None of this is our usual territory, and we would rather say so than pretend otherwise. The Long Second is about hours that stretch; the Olympic clock is about instants that shatter. But Munich left both worlds the same lesson. Past a certain resolution, measurement stops describing the race. Swimming drew its line at the hundredth and let ties stand. A rider on a six-hour day draws it far coarser — the climb went, or it didn't — and loses nothing by the rounding. Precision, it turns out, comes in sizes. The craft is in matching it to the effort.
