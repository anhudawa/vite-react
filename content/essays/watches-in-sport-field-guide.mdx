Sport and the fine watch have circled each other for a century — the chronograph timing
the lap, the diver on the regatta, the brand on the boards behind the podium. Most
coverage stops at identification: a famous wrist, an expensive object, a retail price,
done. That misses the only thing worth knowing.

Because two athletes can wear the identical watch and mean opposite things by it. One was
handed it by a marketing department and is, in the most literal sense, rented
advertising space. The other saw it, wanted it, and spent his own money. Same photograph,
opposite stories. The watch is the easy part. **Whether it was bought or it's a paid
placement is the whole subject** — and once you start watching for it, you see it
everywhere, in every sport.

## Tennis: the watch built to be played in

Tennis is where the worn-in-competition watch reaches its strange peak. Richard Mille
built the **RM 27** line for **Rafael Nadal** — named the first one after him, then
engineered each version lighter and more shock-resistant so he could actually swing in
it. He wore the roughly £850,000 RM 27-05 on court at Roland Garros. That is a paid
partnership, but it is also a watch doing the one job it was built for, on the wrist of
the man it was built for.

**Roger Federer** sits at the other, quieter end — a Rolex testimonee for over two
decades, the dress watch as ambassadorship. Both are "paid to wear it." The interest is
in how differently the same category can read.

## Golf: the corporate heartland

Golf is Rolex country. **Tiger Woods** has been a testimonee since 2011 — one of the
longest-running ambassadorships in the sport — and **Jon Rahm** is on the same roster.
These are placements, plainly: the brand and the athlete in a long, mutually flattering
arrangement, the watch a few tens of thousands of pounds of understatement. Honest, and
worth logging as exactly what it is.

## Cycling: six figures over the cobbles

The most absurd, and therefore most interesting, case in endurance sport. **Tadej
Pogačar** and his great rival **Mathieu van der Poel** both race in the Richard Mille
**RM 67-02** — an extra-flat automatic the brand builds specifically to be raced — over
the pavé of Paris-Roubaix, the most destructive one-day race there is. A six-figure
mechanical movement, soaked and shaken, doing nothing a £300 bike computer doesn't do
better, except survive. Both are paid. Both wear it where almost nobody would risk a
watch a fraction of the price. That contradiction is the point.

<Pull>
  A sponsored athlete in a sponsor's watch is an advertisement. A man buying one with his
  own money is a confession. The second is rarer, and harder to prove.
</Pull>

## Football: where the collectors are

Football is where the bought-vs-paid split gets sharpest. **David Beckham** is Tudor's
first brand ambassador — a paid deal, and the floor of the money range, a few-thousand-
pound Black Bay worn by a man who could buy anything. **Cristiano Ronaldo** is a paid
Jacob & Co. ambassador, but he is also, by his own account, a personal Franck Muller
collector from before any brand paid him.

And then there's **Lionel Messi**. His paid watch deal is with Jacob & Co. But the
Rolexes and the Pateks — the green-dial Day-Date, the Yacht-Master he wore signing for
PSG, the Patek 5270P he wore collecting a FIFA award — those he buys himself. Nobody pays
Messi to wear a Rolex. That makes him one of the most interesting wrists in the game: a
genuine collector who happens to be the best footballer alive.

## Boxing and MMA: the flex, and there's nothing wrong with it

Combat sport is where the money is loudest, and we don't moralise about that — the flex
is a perfectly good reason to buy a watch. **Canelo Álvarez** was filmed at a boutique,
fight week, selecting a Jacob & Co. Opera Godfather and saying, simply, "I want this
one." No ambassador contract, no press release, just the purchase. That is the rarest and
cleanest thing on this whole page: a man buying a watch with his own money, on camera.

## The money, in one range

Lay it out and the spread is the story: a **£3,000 Tudor** on Beckham, a **£40,000
Rolex** on Tiger, a **£150,000 Richard Mille** on Pogačar, an **£850,000** one on Nadal,
seven figures on the wrists of the boxers. The number is never the embarrassment some
coverage treats it as. The number is the doorway.

## So: bought, or paid to wear it?

That's the field guide, and it's really just one question asked in every sport. Is the
watch a placement the brand initiated, or a purchase the athlete chose? A paid deal tells
you what a brand believes about an athlete's reach. A personal purchase tells you what the
athlete believes about the watch — and only one of those is a confession.

The watch is the easy part. Who paid whom is the story.
