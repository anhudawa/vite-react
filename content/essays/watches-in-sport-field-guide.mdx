Freeze any frame from a hard day — the pavé, a climb in the rain, a finish-line collapse —
and somewhere in it is a wrist wearing more than the cameraman earns in a year. Most coverage
stops right there. A famous wrist, an expensive object, a retail price, done. I used to read
it that way too. That is identification, and it misses the only thing worth knowing.

The price tells you almost nothing. The question underneath it tells you everything. Was the
watch handed over, or chosen? A handed watch is rented advertising space. A chosen one is a
rider spending their own money on a thing they wanted. Same photograph, opposite stories. The
watch is the easy part. Whether it was bought or it's a paid placement is the whole subject,
and once your eye learns to read that, it reads across every sport that runs on suffering.

## Cycling: six figures over the cobbles

The most absurd case in endurance sport, which is exactly why it's the one I love. **Tadej
Pogačar** and his great rival **Mathieu van der Poel** both race the Richard Mille **RM
67-02** — an extra-flat automatic the brand builds specifically to be raced — over the pavé
of Paris-Roubaix, the most destructive one-day race there is. I've been spat off lesser
cobbles than those and felt my own teeth in the handlebars; the idea of a six-figure
mechanical movement, soaked and hammered for roughly seven hours, doing nothing a £300 bike
computer doesn't do better except survive, is gloriously hard to defend. Both riders are
paid. Both wear it exactly where no sane person would risk a watch a fraction of the price.
The contradiction is not a flaw in the story. It is the story.

<Pull>
  A sponsored rider in a sponsor's watch is an advertisement. One buying a watch with their
  own money is a confession. The second is the rarer thing, and the harder one to prove.
</Pull>

## The instrument and the keepsake

What makes endurance the cleanest place to read all this is that the real instrument and the
expensive one are never the same object. The thing actually deciding the race is on the bars
or strapped flat to a forearm: a GPS unit, a computer, a multisport watch counting power and
pace and the slow drain of a battery against the slow drain of you. That is the tool. It earns
its place by being useful at hour five, when useful is the only quality that survives.

The mechanical watch on the same wrist is doing something else entirely. It isn't measuring
the effort; it's keeping it. A keepsake worn through the work rather than an instrument of it.
Both can be true at once, and the more interesting wrists hold both — the cheap honest tool
that does the timing, and the costly object that does the remembering. Once you stop expecting
the expensive one to be the useful one, the whole picture settles into focus.

I'll be honest about the edge of what I can prove here. Cycling is the case I can name with
real specifics, riders and reference numbers and a race I've bled on a smaller version of.
Running and triathlon run on the same logic — the GPS watch as the true instrument, the
mechanical as the keepsake, and the same buried question of who paid whom — but I'm not going
to hang a name on a wrist I haven't verified. When I have the specifics, I'll write them.
Until then the category is the honest unit, and the category alone makes the point.

## So: bought, or paid to wear it?

Coverage treats the big number as the embarrassment to get past — six figures of Swiss
mechanism getting hosed down in a feed zone, what a waste. That gets it backwards. The number
is the doorway, and you walk through it to the only question that matters, the one the number
cannot answer for you. Was the watch a placement the brand initiated, or a purchase the rider
chose? A paid deal tells you what a brand believes about a rider's reach, which is useful and
worth logging. A personal purchase tells you what the rider believes about the watch, with
their own money, where nobody made them. Only the second is a confession.

The watch is the easy part. Who paid whom is the story.
