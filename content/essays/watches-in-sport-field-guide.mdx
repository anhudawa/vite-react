Freeze any frame from a final — clay, hardcourt, the pavé, a podium — and somewhere in it
is a wrist wearing more than the cameraman earns in a year. Most coverage stops there: a
famous wrist, an expensive object, a retail price, done. That is identification, and it
misses the only thing worth knowing.

Because the price tells you almost nothing, and the question underneath it tells you
everything. Was the watch handed over, or chosen? A handed watch is rented advertising
space; a chosen one is a man spending his own money on a thing he wanted. Same photograph,
opposite stories. The watch is the easy part. Whether it was bought or it's a paid
placement is the whole subject — and once you can read it, you can read it anywhere.

## Tennis: the watch built to be played in

Tennis is where the worn-in-competition watch reaches its strange peak. Richard Mille built
the **RM 27** line for **Rafael Nadal** — named the first one after him, then engineered each
version lighter and more shock-resistant so he could actually swing in it. He wore the
roughly £850,000 RM 27-05 on court at Roland Garros. A paid partnership, plainly. But also a
watch doing the single job it was built for, on the wrist of the man it was built around,
which is a rarer and more honest kind of placement than most.

**Roger Federer** sits at the other, quieter end: a Rolex testimonee for over two decades,
the dress watch as ambassadorship. Both men are paid to wear it. The skill is in hearing how
differently the same two words can sound — one a watch surviving combat, the other a watch
lending its calm to a name. Paid is not a single story. It is a range.

## Golf: the corporate heartland

Golf is Rolex country, and Rolex country is the cleanest placement there is. **Tiger Woods**
has been a testimonee since 2011, among the longest-running ambassadorships in any sport;
**Jon Rahm** is on the same roster. These are deals, openly — the brand and the athlete in a
long, mutually flattering arrangement, the watch a few tens of thousands of pounds of
deliberate understatement. There is nothing to expose here. The point of the field guide is
not to catch anyone out; it is to log each wrist as exactly what it is. Golf is paid, and
unbothered about it.

## Cycling: six figures over the cobbles

The most absurd case in endurance sport, and therefore the most interesting. **Tadej
Pogačar** and his great rival **Mathieu van der Poel** both race the Richard Mille **RM
67-02** — an extra-flat automatic the brand builds specifically to be raced — over the pavé
of Paris-Roubaix, the most destructive one-day race there is. A six-figure mechanical
movement, soaked and hammered for seven hours, doing nothing a £300 bike computer doesn't do
better except survive. Both riders are paid. Both wear it exactly where no sane person would
risk a watch a fraction of the price. The contradiction is not a flaw in the story. It is the
story.

## Football: where the collectors are

Football is where the split gets sharpest, because football is where the wrists buy for
themselves. **David Beckham** is Tudor's first brand ambassador — a paid deal, and the floor
of the money range, a few-thousand-pound Black Bay worn by a man who could buy the building.
**Cristiano Ronaldo** is a paid Jacob & Co. ambassador, and also, by his own account, a
personal Franck Muller collector from long before any brand wrote him a cheque. The same
wrist can hold both stories at once.

And then there is **Lionel Messi**. His one paid watch deal is with Jacob & Co. But the
Rolexes and the Pateks — the green-dial Day-Date, the Yacht-Master from signing for PSG, the
Patek 5270P he wore collecting a FIFA award — those he buys himself. Nobody pays Messi to
wear a Rolex; the brands don't sign active footballers, and he doesn't need them to. That
makes his the most interesting wrist in the game: a genuine collector who happens to be the
best footballer alive, spending earned money on objects no marketing department chose. Taste,
not strategy.

## Boxing and MMA: the flex, and nothing wrong with it

Combat sport is where the money is loudest, and the loudness is not a problem to apologise
for — the flex is a perfectly good reason to buy a watch, and an honest one. **Canelo
Álvarez** was filmed at a boutique, fight week, picking out a Jacob & Co. Opera Godfather and
saying, simply, "I want this one." No contract, no press release, no brand in his ear. Just
the purchase, on camera. That is the rarest and cleanest thing on this whole page: not a
placement dressed as desire, but desire with a receipt.

## The money is the doorway, not the story

Lay the range out and the spread is the point: a few-thousand-pound Tudor on Beckham, a
Rolex in the tens of thousands on Tiger, a six-figure Richard Mille on Pogačar, an £850,000
one on Nadal, seven figures on the wrists of the boxers. Coverage treats the big number as
the embarrassment to get past. It isn't. The number is the doorway. You walk through it and
arrive at the only question that matters, which the number cannot answer for you.

## So: bought, or paid to wear it?

That is the field guide, and it is finally one question asked in every sport. Was the watch a
placement the brand initiated, or a purchase the athlete chose? A paid deal tells you what a
brand believes about an athlete's reach — useful, and worth logging. A personal purchase
tells you what the athlete believes about the watch, with his own money, where nobody made
him. Only the second is a confession.

The watch is the easy part. Who paid whom is the story.
