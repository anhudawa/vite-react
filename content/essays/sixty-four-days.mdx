At 07:24 universal time on the morning of 14 January 2025, Charlie Dalin sailed MACIF Santé
Prévoyance into the channel at Les Sables-d'Olonne and stopped a clock that had been running
since the tenth of November. It read 64 days, 19 hours, 22 minutes and 49 seconds. Nobody had
ever taken a boat around the world alone, without stopping and without assistance, in less. The
record he broke had stood for eight years, and it fell by more than nine days.

This site has sat with the second, the hour, and [the seventeen-hour day](/features/seventeen-hours). The Vendée Globe is
what a race against the clock becomes when the clock is allowed to run for months. Nothing else
in mainstream sport keeps a single timer going this long, and no other timed event asks its
athletes to live inside the elapsed time rather than merely spend it.

## The open end

[The Hour](/features/the-longest-hour) fixes the time and asks for distance. The Vendée Globe fixes something larger, the
planet, and leaves the time open. The course is simple to say and absurd to do: leave Les
Sables-d'Olonne, keep the three great capes to port, come back. Roughly 24,000 nautical miles,
solo, non-stop, unassisted, every four years. Philippe Jeantot, who had twice won his class in
the BOC Challenge and disliked that it was sailed in stages, founded the race in 1989 as the
version with the stops removed. Thirteen skippers left that November. Seven came back, the
first of them, Titouan Lamazou, after 109 days at sea.

The attrition is the tradition. In 1996–97, sixteen boats started and six finished, and one
skipper, Gerry Roufs, was lost in the Southern Ocean and never found. The boats have grown
safer and the finish rates have climbed — thirty-two of forty starters made it home in 2024–25
— but the race still budgets its drama in weeks, on water where [carrying the right time was
once the only way of knowing where you were](/features/the-clock-that-found-the-ship).

## Sleep, spent in small coins

Every other race on this site fits inside a waking day. A rider can stay conscious for the
whole of an hour, an Ironman, even a seventeen-hour cutoff. The Vendée is longer than
consciousness. No skipper can stay awake to the finish, or even to the next weather system, so
sleep has to move inside the race, broken into pieces small enough that the boat cannot get
into too much trouble without its human. The practice is polyphasic: naps of twenty to forty
minutes, several times a day, for two months. When the chronobiologist Claudio Stampi studied
Ellen MacArthur through the 2000–01 edition, her numbers came back at 5.7 hours of sleep a day,
taken as nine or ten naps averaging 36.6 minutes each.

The boat, meanwhile, keeps racing. The autopilot steers while the skipper is under, which makes
sleep here a strange purchase: not time lost but speed unwatched, a wind shift arriving with
nobody awake to answer it, a sail trimmed for conditions that no longer exist. Sleep too little
and judgment goes. Sleep too long and the boat sails below itself for the length of a dream,
and over sixty-four days those dreams compound into the record or out of it.

## The wake that went cold

A stage race gives you the rival alongside. The Vendée mostly gives you a rival who finished
years ago. When Dalin left in November 2024, the number he was chasing — Armel Le Cléac'h's 74
days and 3 hours, set in January 2017 — had been waiting for eight years, through an entire
edition it survived untouched. A record at this scale is an opponent in the purest sense
available to sport. It cannot crack, cannot get tired, and it stopped racing before some of the
fleet's boats had been built. For two months Dalin sailed against a wake that had long gone
cold, and beat it by nine days.

Then the part nobody watching that January morning knew. Dalin sailed those sixty-four days
with a rare gastrointestinal cancer, diagnosed in late 2023, setting alarms on his phone so the
daily treatment would not slip between the naps and the sail changes. He told almost no one
until his autobiography appeared in the autumn of 2025. He died on 11 June 2026, at forty-two,
seventeen months after stopping the clock.

The race leaves again in 2028. The fleet will do what Vendée fleets have always done, chase a
finisher who is no longer racing — only now the phrase is exact, and the mark it points to is
sixty-four days, nineteen hours, twenty-two minutes and forty-nine seconds. What a man did with
the time he had.
