Three goals past Algeria, and at the final whistle Lionel Messi stood level with Miroslav
Klose as the World Cup's all-time leading scorer. The record is the headline. The detail
the cameras kept finding is on the wrist: a Day-Date Rolex has never printed in a catalogue.

It was a Day-Date 40 in "Jubilee Gold," Rolex's in-house yellow-gold alloy, with a dial cut
from green aventurine — the flecked, deep-space green that throws light like crushed glass.
The reference is 228235JG. Rolex showed it at Watches and Wonders 2026 and makes it in the
quiet numbers it reserves for the clients it most wants to keep. List price is around
$62,700. The six-figure "grey-market value" that follows the watch across social feeds is
not a price; it's a number someone invented about what a flipper might ask, repeated until
it sounds like fact.

## The fact under the fact

Here is the line almost every write-up skips. Messi is not a Rolex ambassador. Rolex does
not pay footballers to wear its watches. His one paid watch relationship is with Jacob & Co.,
and that is the whole of it. The green Day-Date he wore against Algeria, he bought. He is, in
the language of the trade, the kind of client every boutique wants walking through the door —
which is a different relationship entirely from the kind a marketing department signs.

That distinction is the only one this magazine cares about, and it reorganizes everything.
A sponsored athlete in a sponsor's watch is wearing advertising; the wrist has been rented.
A man who quietly acquires one of the hardest references in the world to obtain, and then
straps it on for the most-watched ninety minutes of his year, is doing something the opposite
of advertising. He is spending his own money on an object he wanted, in front of everyone,
for no reason but that he wanted it.

## The collection as a diary

Read the rest of the wrist with that in mind and it stops being a list of expensive things.
The platinum Day-Date he lifted the trophy in at Lusail in 2022. The Everose Yacht-Master
from his PSG signing day. The Patek Philippe watches he wears to awards nights. Set against
the green Day-Date, these aren't four luxury purchases stacked up. They're four entries in a
diary, each one fixed to a date that mattered.

A collection assembled by a brand looks like a campaign: one house, one aesthetic, a name
attached to a roster. A collection a man buys for himself looks like his life. There is a
watch for the night a transfer was signed, a watch for the trophy, a watch for the rooms
where the sport hands out its prizes, a watch for the tournament where he ran down a record.
Nobody planned that sequence as a marketing arc, which is exactly why it reads as a person
rather than a strategy. The taste is unmanaged. That is what makes it taste at all.

Notice, too, that the choices don't stay inside one house. A man under contract wears what
the contract names. Messi spreads himself across Rolex for most of it and Patek Philippe for
the awards nights, with the single Jacob & Co. relationship sitting off to the side as the
one place a brand actually pays him. That spread is itself evidence. A wrist that wanders
between makers is a wrist nobody is steering — picking the platinum Day-Date for one occasion
and a Patek for another because the occasion, not a clause, asked for it. The variety is the
fingerprint of a buyer rather than a billboard.

It also explains why the platinum Day-Date is the one to study hardest. Lusail, 2022, the
single most-photographed moment of his career, and on his wrist sits not his loudest watch
and not anyone's sponsor — a quiet platinum case most of the crowd could not have identified.
No agency modelled that choice. No brand signed off. In the minutes before the photograph
that will outlive everyone who watched it live, a man chose what would go on his wrist for
the rest of history, and chose restraint. That is as close to a pure personal statement as a
watch in sport ever comes.

## Why the buying is the story

It would be easy to make the rarity the point. The green aventurine dial, the off-catalogue
reference, the list price most people will never be quoted in person — all of it is genuinely
extraordinary, and a lazier piece would stop there and call the watch the story. The watch is
not the story. The buying is.

Because the rarity, on its own, says nothing about the man. Plenty of rare watches reach
plenty of wrists by contract, handed over to shift units, worn because a clause requires it.
What separates Messi's green Day-Date from those is not the dial; it is the absence of a deal.
He wanted the rarest thing Rolex was making the year he tied Klose, and he had the standing to
be offered one, and he paid for it. Strip the placement out of a watch and what remains is the
only interesting question a wrist can answer: not how much, but why him, and why this.

So when the cameras find his wrist — and at a World Cup they always do, in the tunnel, at the
whistle, under the confetti — the question worth asking is the one a press release can never
answer. Bought, or paid to wear it? With most athletes, the honest answer is paid. With Messi,
it is almost always bought, and the receipt is the whole point. The watch is extraordinary.
That he paid for it himself is the autobiography.
