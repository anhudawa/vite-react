Hold a mechanical watch to your ear. At the modern standard of 28,800 vibrations per hour — 4 Hz, eight beats a second — the ticking runs almost too fast to count. And the tick itself is a compression: each one is a cluster of tiny impacts, five by the horologist's count, spaced so closely the ear fuses them into a single sound. A timing machine hears them separately. A watchmaker reads their spacing the way [a coach reads splits](/features/the-instrument-of-effort).

That sound is the escapement at work, and the escapement is the part of the watch that makes it a watch.

## The problem of release

A wound mainspring wants to be an unwound mainspring, immediately. Free the barrel with nothing downstream to check it and the gear train spins itself out in seconds — hands blurring, then still. The energy is all there. The problem is release. Anyone who has burned through a marathon's [first 10K at 5K pace](/features/the-same-machine) knows the shape of it.

The escapement solves the problem with two parts. The escape wheel is the last gear in the train, usually fifteen teeth, each tipped with the small flat impulse face of the Swiss club-tooth pattern. Astride it sits the pallet fork, a lever shaped like a ship's anchor, each arm carrying a polished stone of synthetic ruby — one entry, one exit. At the fork's far end, a slotted horn waits for a jewel riding on the balance wheel's roller. The balance is the timekeeper. The escapement is its go-between, its jailer and its servant, roughly in that order.

## Lock, impulse, drop

Watch one beat. A tooth of the escape wheel presses against the entry stone and stops: lock. The whole train stands still behind it, the mainspring's torque held on a face of ruby set just past the line of safety. The balance swings in from its arc; its impulse jewel enters the fork's slot and knocks the lever aside; the tooth slips off the locking face.

Then impulse. Tooth and stone slide across each other's inclined planes — in the Swiss design the push is split between the two faces — driving the fork over, and the fork hands that push back to the balance through the jewel. A flick. A few degrees of encouragement, enough to replace what friction stole. The wheel advances half a tooth space and falls: drop, the sound of the next tooth landing on the exit stone. Locked again. The balance sails away through the rest of its arc, touching nothing, then comes back and does the whole thing mirrored on the other side.

There is a fourth act, quieter: draw. The locking faces are angled — a dozen-odd degrees — so the wheel's own pressure pulls the fork snug against its banking pin between beats. A watch on a wrist gets knocked around. Draw is what keeps a jolt from unlocking the train early.

Eight beats a second is 691,200 a day. The escape wheel, two beats to a tooth, makes a full turn roughly every four seconds. And through all of it the balance touches the escapement only twice a swing — once to unlock, once to take its push. The rest of the arc it is free — detached, in the trade's word — which is the whole point.

## Mudge's lever

The detachment was the insight. In the mid-1750s Thomas Mudge, a London watchmaker, put a lever between the escape wheel and the balance so the oscillator could swing free instead of dragging against the train through its whole arc, as earlier escapements forced it to. In 1770 he built it into a watch that George III bought for Queen Charlotte — it is in the Royal Collection still. Refinement took another century and more — ruby stones, the club-tooth wheel, draw — but by about 1900 the lever was in virtually every mechanical watch made, and it still is. When George Daniels's co-axial escapement reached production in Omega's calibre 2500 in 1999, Omega called it the first practical new escapement in some 250 years. That is how hard this problem is.

## Where the maker lives

The escapement is where watchmaking stops being assembly and becomes adjustment. Pallet stones sit in beds of shellac; a watchmaker warms the fork, nudges a stone by microns, lets it cool, and listens again. Too little lock and the watch trips on a shock. Too much drop and energy leaks away as noise instead of timekeeping. The work is closer to voicing a reed than to engineering, and it is judged the same way — by ear first, then by machine.

There is a plain beauty in a component whose entire duty is release: a part that holds only in order to let go, and lets go only this much, eight times a second, for years, without applause. The balance gets the poetry. The escapement does the counting. And somewhere a watchmaker with an eyeglass and a warm needle of shellac is still deciding, a micron at a time, exactly when to let go.
