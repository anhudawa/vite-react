Lionel Messi put three past Algeria, drew level with Miroslav Klose as the World Cup's
all-time leading scorer, and did it wearing a watch Rolex has never printed in a catalogue.

It was a Day-Date 40 in "Jubilee Gold" — Rolex's in-house yellow-gold alloy — with a dial
cut from green aventurine, the flecked, deep-space green that throws light like crushed
glass. The reference is 228235JG. Rolex showed it at Watches and Wonders 2026 and makes it
in the quiet numbers it keeps for the clients it most wants to keep. List price is about
$62,700. The six-figure "grey-market value" you'll see quoted online is a guess wearing the
costume of a price.

The number isn't the story, though. This is: nobody paid Messi to wear it.

## Football buys; it doesn't get placed

That single fact is what separates the World Cup from almost every other stage in sport.
Golf and tennis are built on rosters. Rolex keeps a stable of major champions; Richard
Mille signs its tennis partners to multi-year deals and builds watches around their names.
So when a world number one wears a watch at Augusta or on Centre Court, the safe assumption
is placement — the watch is there because a contract put it there, and the wrist is, in
part, advertising space the player agreed to rent out.

Football doesn't run on that machinery. Rolex does not sign active footballers. There is no
Rolex football family the way there is a Rolex golf family, no equivalent of the tennis
ambassador model. The biggest names in the game — the ones who would command the richest
endorsement money on earth if the brands offered it — buy their own watches, with their own
money, for their own reasons. Which makes a World Cup the densest concentration in sport of
the rarest kind of watch story: the one nobody was paid to tell.

Messi is the cleanest example of it. He has exactly one paid watch relationship, with
Jacob & Co. Everything else on the long list is his own — this green Day-Date; the platinum
Day-Date he lifted the trophy in at Lusail in 2022; the Everose Yacht-Master from his PSG
signing day; the Pateks he wears to awards nights. None of those was chosen by a marketing
department. They are a man spending money he earned on objects he wanted: the watch as
autobiography, not billboard.

## Why the football wrist is richer

There's a structural reason the football wrist is more interesting than the golf wrist, and
it isn't only the absence of contracts. It's the money, and the culture the money sits in.
Elite footballers are paid like almost no other athletes alive, and they come up in dressing
rooms where the watch is already a fluent language — a way of marking a transfer, a first
real contract, a trophy, the birth of a son. They buy early and they buy often, and they buy
without a brand whispering in their ear. What you get is taste rather than strategy:
collections that actually describe a person.

That is why a green aventurine Day-Date says more than any paid placement could. No one
handed it to Messi to shift units. He wanted the rarest dial Rolex was making the year he
ran down Klose's record, and he had the standing to be offered one. The watch is a fact about
him, not a line in someone's marketing calendar.

## Read the price, then the rumour

The money is half the pleasure here, so it's worth being honest about it. The green Day-Date
lists around $62,700. That is the real, knowable number. The "worth six figures" line that
trails these watches across social media is not a price; it's a vibe — a number someone
made up about what a flipper might ask, repeated until it sounds like fact. We quote the list price
and name the guess as a guess. A watch story that inflates its own numbers is just a press
release with worse manners, and the World Cup wrist deserves better than that.

## The watch you lift the trophy in

There's a sub-genre inside all of this worth naming on its own: the watch a champion chooses
for the single most-photographed moment of his life. In 2022, Messi lifted the World Cup in
a platinum Day-Date. Not his loudest watch. Not anyone's sponsor. A quiet, deliberate pick
for a photograph that will outlive him and everyone who watched it live.

Sit with what that decision actually is. In the minutes before the most important night of a
career, with the whole planet about to freeze the frame, a man chooses what goes on his wrist
for the rest of history. No agency models it. No brand signs off. It is as close to a pure
personal statement as watches in sport ever get — which is exactly why the trophy-lift watch
is the one worth studying hardest.

## What to watch for next time

When the cameras find the wrists again — and at a World Cup they always do, in the tunnel, at
the whistle, under the confetti — there is only one question that matters, and it's the one a
press release can never answer for you: bought, or paid to wear it?

At most events in sport, the honest answer is *paid*. At the World Cup, more than anywhere
else, it is *bought* — real watches, chosen by the men wearing them, photographed by accident
rather than arrangement. That isn't a small distinction. It's the entire reason the World Cup
is the best watch-watching on earth, and it's the lens to bring to every wrist the tournament
puts on screen.
