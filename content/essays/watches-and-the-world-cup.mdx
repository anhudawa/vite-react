For a few weeks every four years, the most-watched event on earth becomes the best
wrist-watching event on earth. A billion cameras find every player, every manager, every
arrival down the tunnel and lift of a trophy — and on those wrists, more interesting
watch stories than a year of red carpets.

The 2026 tournament had an obvious headliner. **Lionel Messi** wore an off-catalogue
**Rolex Day-Date 40 in "Jubilee Gold," with a dial of green aventurine** — reference
228235JG, shown at Watches and Wonders 2026, the kind of piece Rolex hands to a short
list of its best clients in tiny numbers. He had it on around the match where he put
three past Algeria and drew level with Miroslav Klose as the tournament's all-time
leading scorer. Retail is roughly $62,700; the six-figure "grey market value" you'll read
is a guess, not a price.

But the number isn't the story. This is: **nobody paid Messi to wear it.**

## Footballers buy; they don't get paid to wear

Here is what makes the World Cup different from golf or tennis. Those sports run on
ambassadorships — Rolex's golf roster, Richard Mille's tennis partners — so the watch on
the wrist is, more often than not, a placement. Football doesn't work that way. Rolex
doesn't sign active footballers. The biggest names in the game tend to be **personal
collectors**, buying with their own money, which makes a World Cup unusually rich in the
rarest kind of watch story: the one nobody was paid to tell.

Messi is the clearest case. His one paid watch deal is with Jacob & Co. Everything else —
this green Day-Date, the platinum Day-Date he wore lifting the trophy in 2022, the
Yacht-Master from his PSG signing, the Pateks he wears to awards nights — he bought. Those
are his choices, not a brand's.

## The trophy-lift watch

There is a small sub-genre here worth its own mention: the watch a champion chooses for
the single most-photographed moment of his life. When Messi lifted the World Cup in 2022,
he did it in a platinum Day-Date — not a sponsor's watch, not the loudest thing he owns, a
deliberate pick for the photograph that would outlive everything. What a player wears to
hold the trophy is as close to a pure personal statement as watches in sport ever get.
There's no marketing department choosing it. There's just a man and the watch he wanted
on his wrist for the rest of history.

## What to watch for next time

When the cameras find the wrists again, the question is always the same one, and it's the
only one that separates a real watch story from a brand's press release: **bought, or paid
to wear it?** At the World Cup, more than anywhere else in sport, the honest answer is
*bought* — and that's exactly why it's the best watch-watching there is.
