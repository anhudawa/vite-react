There is a photograph of an athlete's wrist, and on it a watch worth more than most
houses. The internet does what the internet does. It identifies the reference, quotes
the retail price, and moves on, satisfied that it has told you something.

It has told you almost nothing. The watch is the least interesting fact in the frame.
The interesting fact is invisible: whether that watch is there because the man chose it
and paid for it, or because a brand is paying him to be seen in it. Same photograph.
Opposite meanings. Nobody checks which one you are looking at.

## The two photographs that look identical

Picture two wrists, the same watch on each. On the first, an athlete who signed a
contract: the watch arrived in a box from a marketing department, the relationship is a
line in a budget, and the wrist is, in the most literal sense, advertising space rented
by the hour. On the second, a collector who saw the thing, wanted it, and spent his own
money — money he could have spent on anything — to have it.

<Pull>
  The watch tells you what it cost. Only the relationship tells you what it means.
</Pull>

These are not the same story. One is a transaction the brand initiated. The other is a
choice the person made. We have a culture fluent in reading the price tag and
completely illiterate in reading the difference, which is strange, because the
difference is the entire point. A paid placement tells you what a brand believes about
an athlete's reach. A personal purchase tells you what the athlete believes about the
watch. Only one of those is a confession.

## Why nobody draws the line

Because it is hard, and because the easy version pays the same. "Athlete wears
£300,000 watch" writes itself from a single photo. "Athlete is *paid* to wear £300,000
watch" requires you to establish the relationship — a sponsorship disclosure, an
ambassador announcement, the absence of one — and establishing things is slow,
unglamorous, and occasionally returns an answer you did not want.

So the line goes undrawn, and a real distinction collapses into a single lazy
category: expensive watch, famous wrist, end of thought. The placement and the
purchase get told as the same story because telling them apart is work and telling them
together is free.

## The honest version is the harder one to prove

Here is the part that ought to bother anyone who claims to cover this seriously: the
flattering version is the easy one to verify, and the truer one is hard. A brand
*wants* you to know its ambassador is its ambassador — it issues the press release, it
publishes the page, it hands you the corroboration. "Paid to wear it" comes
pre-sourced.

"He bought it himself, with his own money," nobody issues a release for. There is no
contract to point at, often no statement, just a pattern of a man turning up in a thing
over years with no one paying him to. That is the claim that takes real work to stand
up — and it is also the one worth the most, because it is the one the subject made
freely.

Which is why we log the relationship as carefully as the reference, and say it in plain
words on every page: bought, or paid to wear. Sponsored, gifted, personal, on loan. Not
because the money is vulgar — the money is the most interesting thing in the photograph
— but because the money only means something once you know which direction it flowed.

The rest of the internet will keep telling you what the watch costs. We would rather
tell you the one thing that actually changes what the picture means: who paid whom.
