A record is a strange kind of object. It is made in a single afternoon — in Paula Radcliffe's
case, the afternoon of 13 April 2003, on the streets of London, where she ran the marathon in
two hours, fifteen minutes and twenty-five seconds. But the making is the easy part to
understand. The hard, almost mystical part is what a great record does afterwards: it sits there,
year after year, while the entire sport throws its fastest women at it and the fastest women
fall short. Radcliffe's number did that for sixteen years. It is the longest any marathon world
record, men's or women's, has stood in the whole post-war era. Most of this site is about
[beating the clock in a moment](/features/the-1989-tour-eight-seconds). This is about the rarer feat — beating it, and then holding the
line against it for a decade and a half.

Sixteen years is the detail to sit with, because it is so far outside the normal life of a
record. Marks at this level usually last a handful of seasons; the sport is always rising, shoes
improve, training sharpens, and the old number gets swallowed. Radcliffe's refused. Athletes who
were children when she set it grew up, turned professional, and ran their own brilliant marathons
straight into the wall of 2:15:25. The clock had recorded a single great day in 2003, and that
recording was still, in 2018, faster than anything any woman on earth had managed since. A
performance does not get more durable than that. It had become less a result than a landmark —
the fixed point the whole event measured itself against.

## When the rulebook blinked

There was one strange interruption, and it belongs in any honest telling. Radcliffe set the
2:15:25 in a mixed race, with male pacemakers helping to tow her along, and in 2011 the governing
body decided that records should, going forward, only count when set in women-only fields. The
logic was real — male pacers are a genuine advantage — but the consequence was almost absurd: it
threatened to strip [the fastest time ever run](/features/the-number-that-doesnt-count) of its status as the *record*, demoting it to a
"world best," and hand the official record to Radcliffe's own slower, women-only mark of 2:17:42.
The sport recoiled at what it had nearly done, and the council reversed course, and the 2:15:25
stayed where it belonged.

That episode is worth more than a footnote, because it draws the line this whole brand keeps
returning to. A rulebook is a human document, and it can hesitate, reclassify, change its mind
about what a performance is allowed to mean. The clock cannot. The clock recorded 2:15:25 and
held it without an asterisk or a second thought, indifferent to the committee meetings happening
around it. Whatever the sport decided to *call* the run, the time itself was never in any doubt.
The number was the one stable thing in the room.

## What it means for a number to last

The marathon is the closest endurance sport comes to [a pure negotiation with time](/features/the-longest-hour). There is no
opponent you can mark, no finish you can sprint from a lap out; there is only the distance and
the hours it takes, and a clock at the end with the whole truth of your day on it. To run 2:15:25
is to win that negotiation once, spectacularly. To have it stand for sixteen years is to keep
winning it, in absentia, against every challenger, for as long as a child takes to become an
adult. The performance kept paying out, year after year, long after the legs that produced it had
retired.

It ended, as these things should, cleanly and in the open. On 13 October 2019, in Chicago,
Brigid Kosgei ran 2:14:04 and took eighty-one seconds off the longest-standing record in the
sport. Radcliffe was there, at the finish, and congratulated her — one holder of the number
handing it on to the next. That is the right image to close on. The record was never really hers
to keep forever; nobody's is. What was hers, and stays hers, is the sixteen years: the decade and
a half in which a single afternoon's running, fixed in a clock at 2:15:25, was simply better than
time could undo. A watch keeps time. Once in a generation, a runner keeps it too.
