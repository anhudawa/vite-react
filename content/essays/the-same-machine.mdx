An escapement releases stored energy in measured beats, and does almost nothing else.
Wind the mainspring and it holds a fixed reserve: no more, no less, a defined number of
turns in a coiled ribbon of steel. Left to itself that spring would empty in one violent
instant. A blur, a whir, a few seconds of frantic motion, then nothing. All the charge
spent at once, and time meaning nothing at all. What the escapement adds is discipline. It
locks the spring's wish to empty itself, permits a single tooth's worth of motion, locks
again, permits another. It is the only reason the watch keeps time instead of simply
ending. The mainspring is the want. The escapement is the rule laid over the want.

I keep coming back to this, because I have been on the other side of it. Consider the
athlete on the start line.

He carries a reserve too, and it is no less physical than a coiled spring. Glycogen in the
muscle. A trained ceiling on what the heart and lungs can move. A tolerance for pain laid
down over a winter of [intervals nobody watched](/features/no-one-at-the-line). Finite to the joule. The race is the
controlled unwinding of all that, and the whole art lives in the word controlled. Go too
early and the spring runs flat before the line, and the last kilometre becomes a slow
public drowning, the body shutting valves it cannot afford to close. Hold too long and you
carry unspent energy across the line, which is the worse of the two failures, because then
it was not the legs that quit but the nerve. The rider who finishes with something left did
not pace conservatively. He miscounted. He was governing a reserve and he got the
arithmetic wrong, and I have been him, and I knew it before the line.

Here is the whole brand, in one observation I am still turning over. A movement and a
marathoner are the same machine, described twice. Both hold a limited charge. Both answer
to a governor whose only job is restraint, releasing that charge in beats rather than in a
burst, turning a violent capacity into an even output that lasts exactly as long as it is
meant to. The watchmaker names the governor an escapement. The coach names it pacing.
Neither borrowed the idea from the other. They arrived, separately, at the same answer to
the same question, which is how you make a finite store of energy last across time without
spending all of it in the first glad rush.

## A tick is a decision

The sound a watch makes is not the sound of time passing. Time keeps no sound, and it would
keep passing in a silent room with no clock in it. The tick is the sound of energy held
back: the escape wheel driving forward, the pallet dropping in to stop it, the brief held
silence, the release of one tooth exactly, the lock again. A refusal and a permission,
several times a second, for as long as it runs. Every tick is the movement declining to
spend everything it has and conceding only the next small increment. A watch says no to
itself thousands of times a minute, and the ticking is the sound of the no.

An athlete's tick is the cadence. Eighty-five revolutions a minute, metronomic, held for
five hours up climbs and down valleys and into a headwind that wants to break the rhythm,
is not a stylistic flourish. It is the same refusal, performed by a body, the same decision
to let the charge out in even beats instead of a flood. Watch a rider who [surges and fades](/features/the-sweep-and-the-surge)
and surges and fades and you are watching a broken escapement: the energy is all there, the
engine enormous, but the governor has gone. The fast-when-wound, slow-when-flat watch and
the attack-and-die rider are one defect under two names. The power was never the problem.
The metering of it was.

## The only lens I trust

The watch world tends to cover these objects as objects: case diameter, movement reference,
dial variant, auction result, the price and the provenance and the waiting list. The sports
world covers the athlete as a result: a time, a place, a gap, a number on a sheet that
settles who beat whom. Both readings are accurate, and both walk past the one thing the two
worlds hold in common, which is also the only thing I want to write about. The relationship
between a running mechanism and a reserve that is steadily, irreversibly draining out.

That relationship is the common ground, and it is why a serious cyclist who has never owned
a mechanical watch and never wanted one will, the first time someone sits him down and
walks him through the escapement, feel an odd flicker of recognition cross his face. He is
not learning something new. He is being handed precise words for something he already knows
in his legs. He has been this machine. He has been the wound spring with the governor
straining over it, in the last kilometre, the reserve nearly flat and the line not yet
close enough, letting out what remained one even beat at a time because the only other
options were to quit or to burst, and he had trained all winter to do neither.

I am still learning the watches. The rest of it I already knew, and so does he.
