An escapement is a device for releasing stored energy in measured beats. Wind the
mainspring and it holds a fixed reserve — no more, no less. Left alone, that spring
would unwind in a single violent instant and time would mean nothing. The escapement
is the discipline imposed on the release. It is the reason a watch keeps time instead
of simply ending.

Consider the athlete on the start line.

He has a reserve too. It was built over a winter of intervals nobody watched, and it
is finite to the joule. The race is the controlled unwinding of it. Go too early and
the spring runs out before the line. Hold too long and you carry unspent energy across
it, which is its own kind of failure — the worst kind, because it is the failure of
nerve rather than of legs.

This is the whole brand in one observation. A movement and a marathoner are the same
machine, described twice. Both store a limited charge. Both are governed by a mechanism
whose entire job is restraint — to release the charge in beats rather than in a burst.
The watchmaker calls it the escapement. The coach calls it pacing. They are describing
the same virtue.

## The tick is a decision

The sound a watch makes is not the sound of time passing. Time does not make a sound.
The tick is the sound of energy being *withheld* — the escape wheel locking against a
pallet, held, and released one tooth at a time. Every tick is a small refusal to spend
everything at once.

An athlete's tick is the cadence. Eighty-five revolutions a minute, metronomic, for
five hours, is not a detail of style. It is the same refusal, performed by a body. The
rider who surges and fades, surges and fades, has a broken escapement. The energy is
there; the regulation is not.

## Why this is the only honest lens

The watch world covers these objects as objects — case diameter, movement reference,
auction result. The sports world covers the athlete as a result — a time, a place, a
gap. Both miss the thing the two share, which is the only thing worth writing about:
the relationship to a reserve that is running out.

That relationship is what an athlete and a fine watch have in common, and it is why a
serious cyclist who has never owned a mechanical watch will, the first time someone
explains the escapement to him, feel a flicker of recognition. He already knows this
machine. He has been it, in the last kilometre, with the spring nearly flat.

The rest is just learning the vocabulary.
