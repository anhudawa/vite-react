The question arrives with every new watch: does it come on the ride, or does it wait on the dresser? Four hours of chipseal, a descent taken badly, one grate hit at 60 kph. You feel all of it in your hands. It is fair to wonder what the movement feels.

The short answer is reassuring. The long answer is more interesting, and less complete than the forums make it sound.

## The part that breaks

A mechanical movement is mostly harder than you think. The wheels, the barrel, the plates — these shrug off abuse. The vulnerable component is the balance staff, the slender axle the [balance wheel](/features/the-escapement-the-part-that-lets-go) swings on. Its pivots are fractions of a millimetre thick and they carry the largest oscillating mass in the watch. Before shock protection, a dropped watch often meant a snapped staff; it was the classic repair of the pocket-watch era.

That changed in 1934, when Universal Escapements of La Chaux-de-Fonds introduced Incabloc. The idea is elegant: mount the jewels that hold the staff on a lyre-shaped spring, so that under impact the whole bearing shifts and a stronger shoulder of the staff takes the blow against solid metal. The force goes around the pivots instead of through them. Rolex's Paraflex (2005) and the Nivachoc system that appeared in Breguet's calibre 777Q in 2006 refine the same principle; Rolex claims Paraflex improves shock resistance by up to 50 per cent, though that figure is the brand's own.

There is even a floor under all this. ISO 1413, the shock-resistance standard, simulates a fall from one metre onto a hardwood floor. To pass, the watch must keep running within 60 seconds a day of its prior rate. That fall is a harder event than anything a bike transmits through your wrist short of an actual crash.

The loads of sport itself have been measured, too. When IWC's research division put numbers on what athletes actually do to a wristwatch, the activities it tested came in at roughly 20 to 60G, and the division's head, Lorenz Brunner, says the 60G of a golf swing gives a mechanical watch — at least one of IWC's — absolutely no problem. Shock, in other words, is a solved and quantified problem.

## Vibration is a different question

Shock is one event. Vibration is thousands of small ones, and here the picture gets thinner.

We know what a bike does. When Sébastien Duc of the University of Reims rode an instrumented bike through the 2015 Paris-Roubaix Challenge, he measured vertical accelerations around 35 m/s² at the stem on the four-star cobbled sectors — hard enough that European occupational action limits for hand-arm vibration were crossed in under three minutes. The cobbles are a measured, violent input.

But that number was taken at the stem, bolted to the frame. A watch rides on your wrist, and the wrist is a damper — skin, tendon, the give of a strap. How much of that 35 m/s² survives the trip through your forearm to the balance wheel, nobody has published.

<Pull cite="THE GAP">No one has strapped an accelerometer to a watch on a rider's wrist across the Arenberg and published the numbers. The honest answer lives between the data we have and the rides we have finished.</Pull>

Over years rather than rides, sustained vibration could plausibly show up as wear — in the rotor bearing, the pivots, the [oils that hold everything in tolerance](/guides/when-to-service-a-mechanical-watch) — but no study has measured that either.

## Cobbles, gravel, tarmac

So the practical taxonomy looks like this. Smooth tarmac on a modern automatic: a non-event. The accelerations are modest, the shock system is loafing, and riders have worn automatics through decades of this without a pattern of failures emerging.

Cobbles and washboard gravel: a real input, hours long, with no good data on what reaches the movement. If the watch is replaceable and serviced, most owners ride anyway and report nothing. If it is not replaceable, this is where doubt is allowed to win.

A crash is the one clear danger, and it endangers the case and crystal before the movement. Worth remembering that the G-Shock exists because the automatic Kikuo Ibe's father gave him shattered on the ground, and Ibe spent two years and more than 200 prototypes building something that could survive a ten-metre fall. For race day on pavé, that is the right tool.

## The verdict most riders land on

Modern automatic, tarmac, [snug strap](/guides/straps-for-sport): wear it. A loose watch bounces, and every bounce is a small shock the spring has to catch; fit is the cheapest protection you own. Vintage piece without shock protection: it stays home, full stop — that balance staff has no spring under it. Cobbles on a watch you love: your call, made knowing the data isn't there.

Mine comes on the ride. It was built to be worn, and the ride is where the wearing happens.
