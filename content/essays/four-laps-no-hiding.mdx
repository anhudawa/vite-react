The gate holds the bike upright at the line. The rider is clipped in, out of the saddle, frozen. The countdown beeps away, the clamp releases — and the race that follows was written before it started, lap by lap, on paper. There is no bunch to hide in and no wheel to follow; the only wind is the one you make.

Track cycling keeps two of these events. The kilo is 1,000 metres alone from a standing start — four laps of a 250-metre velodrome. The individual pursuit is 4,000 for the men, two riders starting on opposite sides of the track, each chasing the other's shadow. In a pursuit final you can win by catching your opponent. Almost nobody does. You win by riding your number.

## Written before it is ridden

A pursuit exists on paper first. Rider and coach agree on a schedule — a target for every lap, planned around a first lap that costs dearly because it starts from zero, then a long aerodynamic settling into rhythm. During the ride the coach walks the line: pacing along the track edge, position and voice telling the rider whether the last split came in up or down on the plan. British Cycling's coach education material calls walking the line the most effective way to communicate in an event that can be won by a hundredth of a second, and teaches coaches to abbreviate the calls — a 16.7-second lap arrives as "sixteen, seven" — delivered just as the rider approaches.

That is the whole tactical universe. A fixed gear, settled before the ride. No brakes. No shifting. The only decision left mid-race is whether to believe the schedule when your body files its objection — which it does, reliably, around halfway.

## Athens, and the axe

The kilo is shorter and crueller. Too short to settle, too long to sprint, it asks for a maximal standing start and then roughly fifty seconds of holding on while the effort turns to concrete. Riders have described the final lap in terms usually reserved for injury.

Its last Olympic night was its best. Athens, 2004: the Olympic record was broken four times in one session. Shane Kelly, Stefan Nimke and Arnaud Tournant each went under the old mark, Tournant leaving it at 1:00.896. Chris Hoy rode last, off the gate with three men already inside the previous record, and beat them all with 1:00.711. Gold by 0.185 seconds. A tenth and a bit, at the end of a kilometre, after everything.

The following year the UCI cut the kilo and the women's 500-metre time trial from the Beijing programme to make room for BMX. Hoy, furious at losing his event, rebuilt himself as a match sprinter and won three golds in Beijing anyway. The pursuit lasted one more Games: after 2008 it was dropped too, folded into the new omnium as the track programme was restructured for London 2012 — a decision protested loudly by riders and writers at the time, and never reversed.

## What the rider gets

From inside, the event is mostly sound. The boom of a disc wheel on the boards, once a lap, where the banking loads and releases it. The coach's call arriving and then gone. In the kilo there is not even that much information — four laps leave barely time for feedback to matter. The rider sees the black measurement line a few metres at a time and holds it, because every drift up the banking is distance ridden for free and paid for in tenths.

The Games let these events go; the clock did not. In August 2021 Ashton Lambie became the first man under four minutes for the pursuit, riding 3:59.930 at altitude in Aguascalientes. Fourteen months later Filippo Ganna went 3:59.636 at the World Championships near Paris — sub-four at sea level, days after he broke the Hour Record. The kilo's mark went the same way: in October 2023 Jeffrey Hoogland took it to 55.433 in Aguascalientes, averaging just under 65 km/h from a standing start. He vomited into a bin on the cooldown. Nobody who has ridden one was surprised.

There is no jury in these events and no luck worth mentioning. The plan was written before the start; the body filed its objection at halfway; the number on the board says which of them was right. Other races hand the rider a placing. These two hand back a measurement — to the tenth.
