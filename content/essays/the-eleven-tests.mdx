In the autumn of 1964, a NASA engineer named James Ragan needed a watch, and he went about it the way an agency goes about anything: he wrote a requirement and sent it to the watch industry. Four companies answered. Hamilton sent a pocket watch — not what anyone had asked for — and was out before a single chamber door closed. That left three wrist chronographs on the bench in Houston: a Rolex, a Longines-Wittnauer, and an Omega Speedmaster, reference 105.003. None of them was a prototype. These were catalogue watches, and the Speedmaster was the same model a club racer could have bought over a counter that week.

Then NASA set about destroying them.

## The protocol

Eleven environmental tests, run between October 1964 and the first of March 1965.

Forty-eight hours at 71°C, then thirty minutes at 93°C. Four hours at minus 18. Fifteen cycles between hot and cold in a near-vacuum. Ten twenty-four-hour rounds at 95 percent humidity, the air swinging between 25 and 70 degrees. Forty-eight hours in pure oxygen at 71°C. Six shocks of 40 g, one from each direction. Acceleration building to 7.25 g, then on to 16. An hour at 1.6 atmospheres, then a vacuum of a millionth of one. Random vibration sweeping from 5 to 2,000 Hz. Finally, thirty minutes of noise at 130 decibels, swept from 40 to 10,000 Hz.

Nobody scores beauty in a protocol. It asks one question in eleven dialects: are you still working?

The man who wrote it doubted anything would be.

## What broke

The heat did most of the killing. The Longines-Wittnauer's crystal warped and gave up its seal against the case. The Rolex stopped in the humidity chamber and failed the high-temperature trial as well. Both of those watches carried the Valjoux 72, a movement with a real racing pedigree. Nothing about the outcome was obvious in advance.

The Speedmaster ran Omega's calibre 321, its Lemania-based column-wheel chronograph, and it kept going. More than that: through all eleven tests it held its rate to roughly five seconds a day.

On 1 March 1965, NASA declared the Speedmaster flight-qualified for all manned space missions — spacewalks included. No launch event, no press tour. A government test report, and a watch that had earned a line in it.

## Outside

Three weeks later, on 23 March 1965, Gus Grissom and John Young carried the first officially qualified Speedmasters into orbit on Gemini 3. Then, on 3 June, Ed White opened the hatch of Gemini 4 over the Pacific near Hawaii and floated out on an eight-metre tether, steering himself with a hand-held manoeuvring gun — the first American to walk in space. The EVA lasted about twenty-three minutes. His Speedmaster was strapped to the outside of the left sleeve of his G4C suit. Outside. In vacuum and raw sun, in the exact conditions the chambers in Houston had spent the winter imitating, with no hull between the watch and any of it.

The photographs from that afternoon are where the whole Moonwatch legend begins. But the legend leans on the part that happened indoors. The Speedmaster got to space through selection: a winter of controlled suffering, designed by a man whose job was to find the breaking point, and a watch that never gave him one.

Maybe that is why the story lands so hard with people who race. We distrust gear that has only been advertised. We trust gear that has been somewhere — the jacket that held through the storm on the ridge, the wheels that came off the cobbles still true. The Speedmaster is the extreme case, a catalogue chronograph that went through the hardest gear review ever staged and came out with a job. Every Speedmaster since carries that winter in it. Wind one on the morning of a long day out and you are setting an instrument that qualified — the hard way, in the dark of a vacuum chamber, months before anyone saw it in the light above the Earth.
