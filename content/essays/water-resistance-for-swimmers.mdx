As a rule of thumb, treat 100m (10 ATM) as the practical floor for actual swimming and 200m or more (20 ATM) for open water and anything serious; 30m and 50m are conventionally splash-and-surface ratings, not swimming ratings. And read every one of those numbers as a tested pressure with a margin built in, never as a depth you'd want to chase.

That last part is where most people get it wrong, so it's worth slowing down on before we climb the ladder.

## What the rating on the dial actually means

The figure stamped on a caseback — 30m, 100m, 200m, or the same thing written as 3, 10, or 20 ATM — is a measure of pressure the watch is built and tested to withstand, not a literal diving depth. That's the ground truth, and it's easy to misread because the unit is metres, which sounds like depth and isn't.

What you're looking at is the result of a static-pressure test. The watch is held still against a set pressure in controlled conditions, and the rating reflects what it held. Still water, steady pressure, a fresh case. None of those three conditions describes you doing a tumble turn.

So the number is honest about what it measures and silent about what it doesn't. It doesn't account for a moving arm pushing a wall of water ahead of it, which briefly spikes the local pressure well above the static figure. It doesn't account for warm water, which can affect gaskets and the tiny pressure differences across a seal. And it describes the watch when the seals were new, not after a few years of the rubber hardening in a sock drawer. The sensible reading is the conservative one: treat the rating as a margin to stay comfortably inside, not a limit to swim up to.

## The practical ladder, hedged honestly

With all of that said, the trade has settled on a set of conventions that work well as rules of thumb. They are not guarantees and they are not a substitute for what a given maker tells you about a given watch. But they'll keep you out of trouble, and they go roughly like this.

A rating around **30m / 3 ATM** is conventionally splash and rain only. Hand-washing, a caught shower, a sweaty run. It is not meant for swimming, and the convention exists precisely because the metres are misleading — three "metres" of static pressure does not survive a real arm stroke.

**50m / 5 ATM** is generally treated as fine for surface contact: a splash, a quick dunk, the shallow end with the kids. Most people in the trade would still not advise it for committed swimming, lap after lap, because that's exactly the dynamic, repeated loading the static figure doesn't speak to.

**100m / 10 ATM** is the conventional practical floor for actual swimming. This is the rung where the rule of thumb says you can train in the pool with a reasonable margin, assuming the crown is shut and the seals are sound. It's the number I'd want before I put a mechanical watch in the water on purpose.

**200m and up / 20 ATM and up** is the convention for diving and for anything serious in open water. It's not that you need to dive to justify it; it's that the extra margin is what absorbs the real-world load that the static number leaves out — the movement, the temperature, the years on the gaskets.

Read that ladder as a set of conventions and lab-tested pressures, not a promise. The point of climbing a rung higher than you strictly need is that the margin is doing the work the number alone can't.

## The open-water and triathlon reality

This is where the gap between the spec and the swim matters most, and where the endurance side of things earns its place in the conversation.

A pool is the kind world. Open water is colder at the start and can be warmer than you'd like at the surface; you're sighting, turning, getting jostled, and your stroke rate climbs when the gun goes. Every one of those is a small argument against trusting the rating at face value. The static test never met a mass swim start.

So for triathlon and open-water work, the convention earns its keep: aim for the higher rungs, 200m and up, and treat the headroom as insurance against exactly the conditions the lab didn't simulate. A screw-down crown matters more here than anywhere — it's the threaded seal that lets a higher-rated watch actually hold under repeated load, and an unscrewed crown undoes the entire rating in one careless minute. Check it's shut before you wade in. Make it a habit, the way you check your goggles.

There's a body-knowledge version of this that any endurance athlete already understands. You don't pace a race at the absolute edge of what you can hold; you leave a margin, because the day will ask for more than the plan did. Water resistance is the same instinct in steel and rubber. The rating is your theoretical ceiling on a perfect day. You want to be swimming well under it.

## The verdict

If you want one number to carry in your head: 100m / 10 ATM is the floor to swim at all, and 200m+ / 20 ATM is what I'd want for open water and triathlon. Hold both as conventions and rules of thumb, not guarantees — the figure is a static-pressure lab test, and your real margin shrinks with movement, with warm water, and with every year the gaskets age.

Do the three unglamorous things and the conventions will serve you well. Buy with more margin than the perfect-day math suggests. Keep the crown shut, screwed down if it screws down. Have the seals checked over time, because rubber doesn't last forever and the rating quietly assumes it does. Respect the number as what it is, a tested pressure read conservatively, and a good watch will keep its end of the bargain for a very long time.
