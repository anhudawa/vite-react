The honest answer is two straps, not one. For anything wet — swimming, rain, the volume a hard session produces — put the watch on rubber, which sheds moisture and rinses clean. For rough dry land, reach for a pass-through nylon strap that breathes and keeps the watch on if a spring bar fails.

Everything else is detail worth knowing, because the strap is the part of the watch that touches the worst of what you do to it. The case can be rated to depths you'll never see; the strap is where the watch meets your skin, your sweat, and the pool, and it's usually the first thing to give up.

## Why bracelets and leather struggle once you sweat

Start with what doesn't work, because it's what most watches arrive wearing.

A metal bracelet is a lovely thing on a dry wrist and a liability on a wet one. The problem isn't the metal; good steel doesn't care about water. The problem is fit and grime. A bracelet is sized in links to your resting wrist, so it can't be cinched tighter the moment you heat up, swell, or want the watch locked down for a swim. It loosens as you cool, the case slides, and the lugs tap the floor when you push off a wall. Worse, the links and clasp are a maze of tiny gaps that collect sweat and salt, and rinsing them means dunking the whole watch and working a brush into the joints. People love bracelets, and so do I. Just not for the back half of a long, wet effort.

Leather is the other default, and it's the worse marriage. Leather is tanned hide, and sweat and water are what tanning spent centuries learning to resist and never fully managing. Soak a leather strap session after session and the fibres swell, the finish cracks, the colour goes blotchy and dark, and it starts to hold a smell you can't get out. A leather strap can be beautiful for years on a desk wrist. Put it through a summer of training and you're shopping for a new one by autumn.

## Rubber: the strap that doesn't mind being wet

Rubber, or modern moulded synthetics in the same family, is the one material here that genuinely doesn't care. Water beads off it. Sweat sits on top instead of soaking in. When you're done you hold the watch under a tap, wipe it, and it's clean, because there's no weave and no pore for anything to lodge in. It dries in minutes, not hours.

It's also the strap you can size to the moment. A pin buckle lets you go a notch tighter for a swim and a notch looser when your wrist swells in the heat, so the case stays planted instead of swimming around. That stability is half of why divers and pool swimmers reach for rubber without thinking. The other half is that there's nothing to ruin: no salt crust forming in a weave, no clasp to corrode, nothing to baby.

The honest trade is comfort and looks. Cheap rubber can feel tacky and trap a film of sweat against the skin; the better stuff is supple and textured underneath to let air and moisture move. Rubber reads as sporty, which is the point here, though it'll look out of place under a cuff at dinner. For water and for pure volume of sweat, nothing else comes close.

## NATO and single-pass nylon: the safety strap

A NATO is a single strip of nylon that runs under the caseback and threads through both spring bars, with a second keeper that sits behind the watch. That construction does one quietly important thing: the strap holds the watch, not the spring bars. If a spring bar fails — and they do, under a knock or with age — a two-piece strap drops the watch on the spot, while a NATO lets the case slide along the band and keeps it on your wrist. For a watch you're going to bash around in, that backstop is worth a lot.

The cost is bulk. The under-pass lifts the watch off your wrist by the thickness of the extra layer, which some people hate and some never notice. A single-pass version threads through both bars without the loop underneath, sitting flatter; you keep most of the comfort and the easy washing, and you give up the captured-watch safety net. Both are woven textile, so they breathe well and dry reasonably, but they soak up sweat and hold it longer than rubber, and salt will dry into the weave if you don't rinse them. For dry-land sport, the trail or the gym or the bike on a clear day, a nylon pass-through is comfortable, cheap, swappable in seconds, and forgiving of abuse. Just don't mistake "water-resistant strap" for "good in the water." It isn't the swim choice.

## Velcro and elastic: breathable, fast, fuss-free

The hook-and-loop and woven-elastic styles, including the fine braided weaves often called perlon, are the comfort specialists. Elastic and perlon breathe better than almost anything else on the wrist, venting as you move, and they dry fast because air passes straight through them. A hook-and-loop strap is the quickest of all to fit, infinitely fine in its sizing, which is useful when your wrist changes size across a long day.

Their weakness is nylon's, a little more so. They're textile, so they drink sweat and want a rinse, and the loop side of a hook-and-loop closure traps grime and slowly loses its grip. They're brilliant for dry, hot, high-output sport where breathability matters most, and a poor idea for the pool.

## So what goes on the watch

Two straps cover almost everything an endurance athlete will throw at a watch. Keep a rubber strap for water and the wettest sessions. Keep a pass-through nylon strap for dry land, where it breathes, swaps in a moment, and keeps the watch on if a spring bar lets go. Add an elastic or perlon band for the most breathable option on hot, dry days, and enjoy the bracelet and the leather for the life you live with dry wrists.

The rule underneath all of it is plain: match the strap to the conditions, not to the dial. A watch is only as ready for your sport as the few centimetres of material holding it on.
