Here is a fact this magazine finds stranger the longer it looks at it. Tadej Pogačar rides
Paris-Roubaix with a Richard Mille worth as much as a house strapped to his wrist. Lance
Armstrong assembled a shelf of sport Rolexes. Breitling built a whole squad around triathlon's
champions. And Eliud Kipchoge — the greatest marathoner who has ever lived, the only human to
cover the distance inside two hours, a man whose entire public identity is a relationship with
the clock — has never had a luxury watch deal in his life.

We went looking, carefully, expecting to find one. There is nothing to find. Before his first
sponsorship he wore a plain Garmin he seems simply to have bought. From November 2020 to
November 2025 he was COROS's ambassador, racing in a plastic GPS watch that sells for a few
hundred dollars — he won his fifth Berlin Marathon wearing a PACE 3 with his own name on the
edition. Since early 2026 his wrist belongs to Huawei, whose Watch GT Runner 2 he helped
develop. Sponsor tools, all of them, and honourable ones. But in the terms this ledger deals
in — mechanical, luxury, the watch as an object beyond its function — the most decorated wrist
in running is unclaimed.

## Why cycling pays and running doesn't

The easy explanation is weight, and it is not wrong. A marathoner at Kipchoge's level treats
every gram as an enemy; nobody who tapers for Berlin is adding a steel chronograph to the
project. But cyclists care about grams too, and Richard Mille solved that problem with money —
a titanium-and-composite movement light enough to race in was the whole point. If haute
horlogerie wanted the marathon, it could engineer its way on.

The truer explanation sits in the cultures. Cycling has always had an aristocratic streak —
the sport of watch deals, team cars, and a peloton that rolls through Europe's old money every
July. Its champions are expected to carry glamour. Marathon running's mythology runs the other
way: the discipline of monkish simplicity, high-altitude camps, one pair of shoes, a training
diary. A six-figure watch on a marathoner's wrist would read, to that culture, almost as a
confession. The plastic watch is not an absence of taste. It is the uniform.

## The open question

Still — sit with what is being left on the table. Watch brands pay serious money to stand near
people who embody precision, endurance, and time. Kipchoge is the most complete embodiment of all
three alive, a man whose Vienna run exists because a clock read 1:59:40, and whose philosophy
("no human is limited") is already half an advertising campaign. Rolex signed swimmers in 1927.
Omega owns the Olympic finish line. Tissot counts the Tour. The marathon's greatest figure,
meanwhile, has spent his career wearing whichever GPS company signed the team.

Maybe the industry is right and the marathon's austerity can't carry a luxury story. Or maybe
the first brand to understand that wrist will get, for less than it pays a mid-tier tennis
ambassador, the most credible endorsement in the history of sports watchmaking. Either way, the
ledger records the present honestly: the fastest marathoner alive, no luxury relationship,
sponsor GPS on the wrist, nothing else to log. Sometimes the most interesting entry is the
empty one.
