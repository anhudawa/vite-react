Tomorrow afternoon in Barcelona the Tour de France begins with a team time trial:
19.7 kilometres through the city, eight riders per squad against one clock, the first
time the race has opened that way since 1971 and only its third start on Spanish soil.
It runs to Paris on 26 July. Tissot's cameras will read the finish to the thousandth of
a second, and this magazine, as usual, will be looking slightly to the side of all
that. We will be watching wrists. Here is the form guide for the next three weeks.

## The verified pair

Start with the two relationships this ledger has checked to its own standard.
[Tadej Pogačar](/who-wears-what/tadej-pogacar) races in a Richard Mille RM 67-02, the
extra-flat automatic supplied through the brand's UAE Team Emirates sponsorship — some
£150,000 of titanium and carbon composite at around 32 grams, built to be worn at speed
and actually worn at speed. He has taken it over the cobbles of Paris-Roubaix. Three
weeks of July are, by that measure, gentle use.

Mathieu van der Poel wears the same reference through a personal partnership signed in
January 2025, and he is confirmed on the start line, leading Alpecin–Premier Tech's
stage hunt alongside Jasper Philipsen. Expect the watch the way you expect the number
on his back. When he and Pogačar mark each other in the mountains, two of the most expensive
wrists in sport will sit inches apart and neither rider will glance down. The timing
lives in the bar-mounted computer. The watch is there to survive.

## The one that stays in the safe

The newest Richard Mille story of this Tour is a watch that will not be raced. Days
before the start, the brand and Colnago, Pogačar's bike maker, launched the RM 64-01
Tourbillon Colnago: fifty pieces, around 800,000 Swiss francs, Pogačar modelling it
through the press images. Colnago then confirmed he won't wear it in the race, citing
personal safety — a caution with history behind it, since Mark Cavendish had two
Richard Milles taken in a robbery at his home in 2021. Somewhere north of a certain
number, a watch stops reading as kit and starts reading as a target. The RM 67-02
rides. The tourbillon waits at home.

Cavendish himself is this Tour's missing wrist. He retired in November 2024, winning
his final race in Singapore with thirty-five Tour stage wins behind him, and whatever
Richard Mille he wears this July, he wears it as a spectator.

## The new signing

The freshest deal is two days old. Paul Seixas — nineteen, leader of Decathlon
CMA CGM, the youngest Tour starter since 1937 — announced a Breitling partnership on
2 July, the eve of his debut. "Every second counts — on the bike, just like on the
wrist," ran his line in the announcement — a press-office sentence over a real deal,
reported by two outlets. What nobody has named is the watch. No reference appears in
the coverage, and whether a Breitling shows up in the race itself or only around it is
one of the things July will settle. We log the relationship now and the model when we
can see it. Breitling has been circling cycling for a while — its recent Top Time
chronograph honoured Eddy Merckx — and claiming the sport's most-discussed teenager on
Tour eve is intent, declared the way luxury declares it: pick the wrist first, let the
story arrive.

## The clock underneath

None of these watches will time a single second of the race. Tissot does that, as it
first did from 1988 and has without a break since 2016: transponders, photo-finish
cameras, synchronisation the company states to the thousandth of a second. The margin
that settled [the 1989 Tour was eight seconds](/features/the-1989-tour-eight-seconds)
after three weeks of racing; the equipment has sharpened since, and the stakes have not
moved an inch. Tissot marks this edition with the PR 100 Tour de France 2026, a quartz
chronograph at about €475 — the official watch of a race whose defending champion is
contracted to one costing three hundred times more. Both facts are true at once, and
the distance between them is most of what this magazine writes about.

## The tan line

And the rest of the bunch? Mostly nothing. The honest inventory of a Tour peloton is
bare forearms — a strip of pale skin where a strap would sit, because the computer on
the bars does everything the race requires and grams are grams.
