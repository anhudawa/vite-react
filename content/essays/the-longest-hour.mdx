Almost every record in sport is built the same way round. You fix the distance — a mile, a
marathon, a hundred metres — and you ask how little time a body can cover it in. The clock is
the judge at the end, counting down the achievement to a hundredth. The cycling Hour Record
turns that on its head, and in doing so becomes the most honest event there is. It fixes the
*time* — exactly sixty minutes, not a second more — and asks how far you can get before it
runs out. The clock is not the judge here. The clock is the opponent.

There is nothing else in the race. No rivals to read, no wheel to sit on, no sprint to time, no
descent to recover on. A rider rolls onto a velodrome alone, the bike is held, and then for one
hour there is nothing but the rider, the boards, and a number ticking toward zero. You cannot
go out hard and hang on, and you cannot wait and pounce. You have to find, on the first lap, the
exact pace that will bankrupt you precisely as the hour closes, and then hold it while your body
spends the next sixty minutes begging you to stop. Eddy Merckx, who won more or less everything
a cyclist can win, rode the Hour in Mexico City in 1972, covered 49.431 kilometres, and called
it the hardest single effort of his life. He never attempted it again. The man was not short of
courage. The Hour is just its own kind of cruelty.

## What the clock holds steady

What makes the Hour beautiful, to a site that thinks about time for a living, is that the clock
is the one variable removed. Every holder of the record — Merckx in '72, Bradley Wiggins in a
London velodrome in 2015, Victor Campenaerts at altitude in Mexico in 2019, Filippo Ganna in
Switzerland in 2022 — was handed exactly the same sixty minutes. Not roughly the same. Not the
same to within a margin. The identical, non-negotiable hour. Everything else can change — the
bike, the air, the altitude, the man — but the hour is constant, which is what lets you lay
these riders' efforts side by side across fifty years and read them honestly. Merckx's 49.431
and Ganna's 56.792 are separated by half a century of bikes and tracks and science, but they are
separated by the same hour, and that shared hour is the only thing that makes the comparison mean
anything at all.

The sport has thought hard about exactly this. For years there were really two Hour records: the
absolute distance, set on radical aerodynamic bikes, and the "Merckx" hour, ridden on equipment
close to what he used, so that the comparison stayed about the engine and not the machine. When
Chris Boardman rode 56.375 kilometres in 1996 in a tucked aero position, that mark was eventually
filed under "best human effort" rather than the Hour proper, and the record was reset to Merckx's
terms. Only in 2014 did the UCI fold it back into a single record on modern pursuit bikes. All of
that wrangling is, underneath, an argument about how to keep a measurement fair across time — the
same instinct that makes a chronometer standard worth having. You hold as many variables steady
as you can, so that the number, when it comes, is telling the truth about the thing you actually
meant to measure.

## Why a watchmaker stands at the rail

It is not an accident that the Hour Record runs under Tissot's name, and that Ganna set the
current mark of 56.792 kilometres at the Tissot Velodrome in Grenchen on a Saturday in October
2022. The same Tissot stood at the finish of the 1989 Tour de France and counted Greg LeMond's
eight seconds. A watch company belongs at an event like this for a reason deeper than a logo: the
Hour is the one race that is *made of* time, where the result is not "who won" but "how much
ground a human could buy with sixty minutes," and that question has no meaning at all unless the
sixty minutes are kept with absolute, unflinching precision. Lose a second of the hour and you
have not made a small error. You have changed the question.

So the rider goes round, and round, and the lap board counts up while the clock counts down, and
the two numbers race each other to the meeting point that is the whole event. When the hour ends,
a hooter sounds, the rider freewheels up the banking utterly emptied, and the distance is read
off — the exact ground that one human covered against the one opponent nobody has ever beaten.
You can ride faster than the man beside you. You cannot ride faster than the hour. You can only
see how far you get before it takes the bike away from you.
