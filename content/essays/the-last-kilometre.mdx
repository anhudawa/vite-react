There is a moment in a bike race that every rider feels in the body before the eyes confirm it:
the flamme rouge, the red kite that hangs over the road at one kilometre to go. It is the
signal that the waiting is over, that whatever you have left has to come out now. You can theme
a watch to cycling in a hundred lazy ways — a peloton on the dial, a brand name borrowed from a
famous climb — but if you want to know whether the people who made it have actually raced, look
at what they chose to put at twelve o'clock. Bravur put the flamme rouge there: a single
triangular index, [lit with Super-LumiNova](/guides/lume-and-the-dark), sitting where a watch usually marks the top of the
hour. That one decision tells you everything about the Grand Tour Sprinter.

This is a small Swedish maker — the watch is hand-built in Båstad, on the south-west coast — and
the piece is, underneath the storytelling, a serious object. A Swiss Sellita SW511b automatic
chronograph beats inside it at 4 Hz with a power reserve of up to sixty-two hours; the case is
38.2 millimetres of 316L steel, [modestly sized in the modern way](/guides/sizing-a-watch-for-a-lean-wrist), water-resistant to 10 ATM,
under a domed sapphire crystal with a sapphire back. None of that is exotic, and none of it is
trying to be. It is a well-specified mechanical chronograph, the kind of honest movement a watch
at around $2,550 should have, and the brand has spent its budget where it matters rather than on
a number to boast about.

## A theme, or a tell

What lifts it is that the cycling is not painted on; it is built in, at the level of detail only
an obsessive gets right. The dial is a muted green with a tarmac texture and three different
shades of purple for the sub-dials — a colourway that sounds mad written down and resolves, in
the metal, into something that looks like a road surface at dusk. There is an inverted numeral
13 on the dial, because riders have worn the number 13 upside down for luck since the early days
of the Tour, a superstition so specific that putting it on a watch is a kind of password: if you
know, you know. The chronograph counts the things a rider counts — a fifteen-minute register for
the short, violent efforts, a twelve-hour counter for the long days in the saddle.

Then you turn it over, and the storytelling keeps going where almost nobody bothers to look. The
case back carries a recessed checker pattern — the finish line — and the rotor, the oscillating
weight that [winds the mainspring](/guides/automatics-on-the-bike) every time your wrist moves, is engraved with the same finish
line, sweeping across it with every pedal-stroke of the arm. It is a lovely, slightly absurd
idea: the part of the watch that turns your motion into stored energy is shaped like the line you
spend a whole race trying to reach first. You will rarely see it. That is exactly why it matters.
A detail you have to go looking for is a detail made for the owner, not the photograph.

## Why this is the harder thing to do

It is genuinely difficult to theme a watch to a sport without embarrassing yourself, and most
attempts fail in the same direction — too literal, too loud, a costume rather than a watch. The
Grand Tour Sprinter works because every reference is the kind a rider would make to another
rider: quiet, specific, and load-bearing. The flamme rouge is not captioned. The upside-down 13
is not explained. The tarmac dial does not announce itself as tarmac. You are simply trusted to
recognise your own world, and a watch that trusts you that way is rarer and better than one that
shouts its concept across the room.

That is the whole reason a brand like this belongs on a site like this. We spend most of our time
on the watches the very best in the world are paid to wear, six-figure pieces loaned for a season
and a photoshoot. The Grand Tour Sprinter is the opposite end of the same love: a watch made by
people who ride, for people who ride, priced so an actual amateur racer might actually own one. It
does not need a sponsored wrist to justify it. It needs a buyer who has heard the kite snap
overhead at a kilometre to go, felt the bottom drop out of the bunch, and wanted, ever after, to
carry a little of that on the wrist. For that person, the last kilometre is the best place on the
dial to start.
