Mercedes Gleitze swam the English Channel on 7 October 1927, the first British woman
to do it. That is the achievement people tend to reach for, and it deserves its own
line, set apart, before anything else gets stacked on top of it: a body in open cold
water for the length of an afternoon and a night, finishing under its own power. Hold
that fact on its own for a moment, because the story I want to tell is not quite that
one, and the two get folded together so often that the more interesting truth goes
missing in the seam.

What happened next was a dispute. A rival had claimed the same crossing, and the claim
was contested, and the contest threw a shadow back over Gleitze's swim that she had not
asked for and did not deserve. So on 21 October 1927 she went back into the water to
answer it — a second swim, undertaken to vindicate the first. The records call it the
Vindication Swim, and the name is honest about what it was: not an attempt at distance
for its own sake, but a public act of proof, performed under witness, to settle a
question that words alone could not.

This is the swim with the watch in it.

## The thing on the ribbon

Around her neck, on a ribbon, Gleitze carried a Rolex Oyster — the early waterproof
case, the sealed thing Rolex had been building toward and now needed to demonstrate
somewhere that mattered. A swimmer going back into open water under the eyes of the
press is exactly that kind of somewhere. The watch went into the water with her and it
came out keeping time. The case had held.

It is worth being precise about what that means, because precision is the whole point
of the object. A [waterproof case](/features/the-waterproof-watch-and-the-open-water) is a claim a manufacturer makes, and a claim is cheap
until something tests it. You can seal a watch on a bench and assert that it is sealed.
What you cannot do on a bench is hand it to a long-distance swimmer, send it into the
cold for the duration of a hard swim, and have the world watch whether it survives. The
swim was [the test rig](/features/the-eleven-tests). Gleitze was carrying the proof, and her body was the conditions.

## The front page

After the swim, Rolex ran an advertisement on the front page of the Daily Mail, and
Gleitze became the company's first sporting ambassador. Sit with how early that is. The
arrangement we now take entirely for granted — an athlete's name and a watch's name set
beside each other, the performance vouching for the object and the object dignifying the
performance — begins, near enough, here. A swimmer, a sealed case, and a national
front page. Most of what the watch industry has done with sport since is a variation on
a move first made in the autumn of 1927.

I find I want to slow down on the honesty of it, too, because the brand I am writing for
is built on getting this right. The often-repeated version of the story collapses the
two swims into one and puts the watch around Gleitze's neck during the first Channel
crossing, the famous one, on 7 October. That is the cleaner legend and it is the wrong
one. The watch moment is the 21st, the Vindication Swim. The crossing proved the
swimmer; the second swim proved the watch. Conflating them robs both of their actual
shape — and robs Gleitze, in particular, of the distinction between a thing she did for
herself and a thing the watch was there to witness.

## What the second swim was for

There is a reason the Vindication Swim, not the crossing, is the founding story of a
watch-and-endurance brand, and it is not only that the watch happened to be present for
the second one. It is what the second swim was *for*. The crossing was an effort with a
destination. The vindication was an effort with a witness — a performance whose entire
purpose was to be seen and believed, to convert a private truth into a public one that
could not be argued with afterward. That is also, exactly, what a waterproof case has to
do. It has to make a claim survivable in front of people who will check.

So the swimmer and the watch were doing the same work that day, from two sides. She was
in the water to prove that the thing she said happened had happened. The case was on the
ribbon to prove that the thing the maker said it could do, it could do. Two claims, one
body of cold water, both settled the only way claims about endurance are ever really
settled, which is by going out and doing the thing where it can be watched.

I am still learning the watches; I came to all of this from the other side, from the
swimming and the suffering and the long efforts that only mean something if someone can
verify the result. Which is maybe why this particular story is the one I keep returning
to. The first marriage of [a watch and an athlete](/features/the-model-from-feat-lineage) was not a logo on a strap or a name in
an advertisement, though the advertisement came. It was a question of proof, asked in
cold water, answered by a woman who had already proven herself once and was made to do
it again — with a sealed case along for the ride, getting its own answer at the same
time. The endorsement was real because the test was real. Everything since has been
trying to remember that.
