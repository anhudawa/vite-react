George Mallory carried a heavy name up Mount Donna Buang in 1994. His grandfather had vanished on Everest seventy years earlier. The grandson, training for his own expedition to the mountain, rode eight laps of the 1,069-metre climb outside Melbourne until he had ascended, on a road bike, the height of Everest. There was no file. No tracker, no leaderboard, no timestamp anyone could audit. A bike, a hill, and afterwards, his word.

The ride sat in obscurity for almost two decades. Then it was written up in 2012, and a Melbourne cyclist named Andy van Bergen read the account and could not leave it alone. In February 2014 his club, Hells 500, sent sixty-five riders into the dark to launch the idea as a challenge with a name and a rulebook: Everesting. Climb 8,848 metres in one activity. Forty finished.

## Rules for a race with no gun

Read the current Everesting rules and notice what they are mostly about. Not speed. Shape. One hill or mountain per ride. Every repeat a full ascent, and back down the same road unless you are prevented from using it. No sleeping. Meal breaks are allowed, but the elapsed clock runs through your sandwich. No "bowl" courses that bank a descent's momentum into the next climb — the elevation has to come out of you. And the rule that holds the structure up: the attempt must be recorded on Strava and submitted, so the crew behind the challenge can check it against the book before your name goes in.

Trail running built the same courthouse. At fastestknowntime.com, the registry of record efforts on named routes, verification means the data: an original GPS file — GPX preferred, pulled straight from the device rather than through a third-party tool — or a link to the activity, plus, ideally, a trip report and photos with their metadata intact. Time is elapsed, not moving; the clock does not pause because you did. Efforts sort into three styles — unsupported, self-supported, supported — defined by what help you accepted along the way. For premier routes and anything expected to run past thirty hours, you are asked to declare your start and share a live tracker before you begin. And the site is plain about its limits. It will not publish a record without satisfactory verification. It also concedes it cannot definitively verify every submission it receives.

## The instrument belongs to the athlete

Official time has always been someone else's property. A stopwatch in a steward's hand, a beam across a line, a chip mat chirping at the finish. The athlete runs; the institution times. That surrender of the clock is most of what makes a sanctioned record a record.

Everesting and the FKT run without it. No line, no official, no gun. The timekeeper rides in your jersey pocket or on your wrist — bought by you, started by you, stopped by you. What replaces the institution is a file and a community willing to read it closely: the review crew, the route page, the comments underneath.

And the file can fail you. In June 2020, Lachlan Morton, a WorldTour professional, rode 42 laps of a hill near Fort Collins, Colorado, and believed he had Everested. His tracking app had under-counted. He was roughly 1,500 feet short of the summit he thought he had built. "I realized pretty quickly I was going to have to go and do it again," he said. A week later he rode 47 ascents of Rist Canyon in 7 hours, 29 minutes and 57 seconds — the fastest Everesting on record at the time. Then he rode one more lap. Insurance.

That extra lap is the whole culture in a single gesture. An official clock ends arguments; a personal one starts them. So the athlete absorbs the doubt himself and answers it with two hundred more metres of climbing that count for nothing except certainty.

This is why the amateur record still feels clean, even now, even at scale. Mallory's word was enough in 1994 because the ride was never really for anyone else. The file has not replaced that word. It stands beside it, the way a second witness does.

Somewhere past hour eight of a ride like this, the numbers on your wrist stop being evidence and go back to being what they were before any of it: a private conversation between you and the effort. The file is for the crew. The time is for you.
