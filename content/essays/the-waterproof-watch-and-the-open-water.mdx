In 1925 a wristwatch was a fair-weather instrument. Rain found it on a long ride. Sweat found it under a sleeve. The sea ended it. The movement inside might be a fine piece of work, capable of years of accurate running, and none of that mattered once moisture crossed the case wall. Dust was slower but just as sure. The problem was never the machine. The problem was the box.

## Three ways in

A watch case of the period had three openings. The bezel that held the crystal. The back that gave access to the movement. And the crown — the one that mattered most, because it had to move. A winding stem is a rotating shaft passed through the wall of the case, and a shaft that turns cannot simply be soldered shut. Casemakers had worked the first two problems for decades. François Borgel's screwed case, patented in Geneva in 1891, threaded the movement into the case from the front. Charles Depollier's waterproof case passed testing by the US National Bureau of Standards, and in 1918 the US Army Signal Corps ordered it in quantity. Tavannes sold a watch called the Submarine. The joints could be managed. The crown stayed a compromise — a sliding fit, a little packing, hope.

## Two names on a patent

On 30 October 1925, in La Chaux-de-Fonds, Paul Perregaux and Georges Perret registered Swiss patent 114948: a crown that screwed down against a threaded tube on the case, clamping the last opening shut. Hans Wilsdorf, the founder of Rolex, acquired the rights. He also inherited its flaw. The design had no clutch — crown and stem turned as one — so a fully wound watch could not be screwed back down and sealed. The version Rolex registered on 18 October 1926 put a clutch in the crown, letting it turn free of the stem as it screwed home. That patent, and the case built around it, is the Oyster.

## Sealed like its namesake

The 1926 Oyster was a system, not a single trick. Bezel, caseback and crown all threaded down against the middle case, compressing the assembly into one sealed body. The fluting on the bezel gave the tool its grip when the case was screwed home; decoration came later. Wilsdorf chose the name himself, and his reasoning survives in a line still quoted a century on.

<Pull cite="WILSDORF">Like its namesake, it could remain under water for an unlimited time without detriment.</Pull>

He understood what he had. This was more than a drier watch. It was a watch that could go where its owner went, including into the water.

## Proof by swimming

A sealed case is a claim. Wilsdorf's insight was that the claim needed a witness, and the best witness was a body in open water. In October 1927 the swimmer Mercedes Gleitze carried a small gold Oyster through more than ten hours in the English Channel, and the watch came out running and dry. Rolex took the front page of the Daily Mail the following month to say so. Her story is larger than the watch's — the record, the doubters, the vindication swim, the cold — and we have told it on its own terms elsewhere. What belongs here is the method. Rolex did not prove the Oyster in a pressure tank and publish the figures. It proved the case on a human being doing one of the hardest things a human can do in water, for hours, in public. The endurance athlete was the test bench. That template — instrument validated by effort — is most of what this magazine cares about.

## What the seal opened

The Oyster was not the first waterproof watch, whatever the advertising said then or the brand literature says now. Borgel, Depollier and others got real water resistance to market earlier, and watch historians still argue over how much credit each deserves. What the Oyster settled was the whole problem at once — crown included — in a watch a swimmer could buy, backed by proof no certificate could match. Every screw-down crown on every dive watch descends from that October 1926 patent. So does something less mechanical: the assumption, now unremarkable, that your watch enters the water with you. A triathlete rolling off a pontoon at dawn gives no thought to the case wall. That silence is the achievement. The water is the same water it was in 1925. The box changed.
