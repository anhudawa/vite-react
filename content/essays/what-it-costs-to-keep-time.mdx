There is a photograph from a recent Tour stage of a rider's wrist, mid-effort, the
hand loose on the tops, and on it a Richard Mille worth more than the team car. The
watch is doing nothing. It is not timing the stage; the bike computer does that, and
does it better. The watch is there to survive the cobbles, which is the only thing it
is genuinely good at that the moment requires.

A few hundred miles away, a marathon is won in a plastic GPS watch that costs less than
the winner's shoes. It is doing everything. Pace, lap, heart rate, the splits that
decide whether the world record falls. It is the most useful object in the race, and
nobody will photograph it.

This is not a contradiction to resolve. It is the subject.

## Two kinds of accuracy

The Coros keeps time the way a tool keeps time: by counting, electrically, against a
quartz reference it trusts completely. It is right to within a rounding error and it
does not care that you admire it.

The Richard Mille keeps time the way a craftsman keeps a promise: imperfectly, through
a hundred small parts that must agree, regulated by an escapement that a person adjusted
by hand and signed off on. It is *less* accurate than the plastic one. That is the
point of it. You are not paying for accuracy. You are paying for the difficulty of
having achieved it mechanically at all.

<Pull>
  The cheap watch tells you the time. The expensive one tells you what it cost to keep
  it.
</Pull>

## The athlete already understands this

Ask a cyclist why she rides a steel frame she built up by feel when a faster carbon one
exists, and she will give you the watch collector's exact argument, in different words.
Efficiency is not the only value. There is a value in the made thing, in the evidence
of a hand, in the deliberate acceptance of a harder path because the harder path is the
one that means something.

The endurance athlete is the one audience on earth that needs no convincing on this.
They have already chosen the difficult version of everything. They climbed the
mountain when the tunnel was right there.

So when we cover a £300,000 watch on a rider's wrist, we are not flexing the price. We
are noting that two finite reserves — the rider's and the mainspring's — are climbing
the same mountain, and only one of them was strictly necessary. The unnecessary one is
the interesting one.
