A rider crests a climb mid-Tour, hand loose on the tops, and on his wrist is a Richard
Mille worth more than the team car idling at the foot of the mountain. Watch the watch and
you notice it is doing nothing. It is not timing the stage; the bike computer does that,
and does it better. It is there to [survive the cobbles](/features/the-overbuilt-watch), which is the one thing the moment
asks of it that it is genuinely good at.

A few hundred miles off, a marathon is won in a plastic GPS watch that costs less than the
winner's shoes. That one is doing everything. Pace, lap, heart rate, the splits that decide
whether the record falls. It is the most useful object in the race. Nobody will photograph
it.

I don't think this is a contradiction to resolve. I think it is the subject.

## Accuracy stopped being the question

For most of the wristwatch's life, the expensive watch and [the accurate watch](/guides/what-a-chronometer-actually-is) were the
same object. You paid more, you got closer to true. That world is gone. A cheap quartz
movement now keeps time better than the finest mechanical one ever made, and a GPS watch
that disciplines itself against an atomic clock in orbit is closer to true than anything on
any collector's wrist. By every measure the word "accuracy" actually means, the plastic
thing on the marathon winner is the better instrument. I had to sit with that for a while.

So the buyer of the £300,000 watch is not buying accuracy. He cannot be. The market made
accuracy almost free and handed it to the cheapest object in the room. Whatever the price
is buying, it is something else, and the whole question of value in watches opens the moment
you accept that the obvious answer is wrong.

## Two kinds of keeping time

The Coros keeps time the way a tool keeps time, by counting, electrically, against a
reference it trusts completely. It is right to within a rounding error and it does not care
that you admire it. Switch it off and nothing is lost but the battery's patience.

The Richard Mille keeps time the way a person keeps a promise: imperfectly, through a
hundred small parts that have to agree, regulated by [an escapement](/features/the-escapement-the-part-that-lets-go) a human being adjusted
and finished by hand. It is *less* accurate than the plastic one. I love that this is not
the flaw in it. It is the whole of it. You are not paying for the time. You are paying for
the difficulty of having coaxed a verdict on the time out of metal at all, for the fact
that someone solved, mechanically, by feel, a problem a battery now solves for nothing.

<Pull>
  The cheap watch tells you the time. The expensive one tells you what it cost to keep it.
</Pull>

That is the line between the two climbs on the mountain, and the place where my two loves
meet. The rider is paying out glycogen and will, hour over hour, with no way to stop the
spending. The mainspring, wound that morning, is paying out too, all day, against the same
hill. One of those climbs is strictly necessary. The other is a man and a brand
agreeing to carry an unnecessary difficulty up a mountain because the difficulty is the
point. The unnecessary one is the interesting one. It always is.

## The athlete already understands this

Ask a cyclist why he rides a steel frame he built up by feel when a faster carbon one sits
in the same shop, and he hands you the watch collector's argument back, word for word, in
different clothes. Efficiency is not the only value. There is a value in the made thing, in
the visible evidence of a hand, in choosing the harder version on purpose because the harder
version is the one that means something. He climbed the mountain with the tunnel right
there. He is not about to be confused by a watch that does the same.

This is the part I keep returning to. The serious athlete is the one audience on earth that
needs no convincing here. The rest of us are taught to ask what a thing is *for*, and to
distrust any answer that isn't use. The athlete has already spent a career proving that the
useful path and the meaningful path are different roads, and that the second one is worth
more, paid for in a currency, suffering, that shows up on no spec sheet. A watch that costs
more than it can ever earn back in function is not an affront to that person. It is a
familiar idea in a smaller package.

## What the price is actually for

So when a six-figure watch turns up on a wrist that does not need it, the price is not an
embarrassment to wave away. It is the evidence. It is the measure of how much difficulty a
person was willing to buy when the easy version was free and lying right there on the next
wrist over.

Quartz won the argument about time. It won so completely that keeping time stopped being
worth paying for, and the whole market reorganised itself around the only thing left to
sell: the cost of having kept it the hard way. The plastic watch on the marathon winner is
the proof of how cheap accuracy got. The Richard Mille on the rider is the price of refusing
the discount. Two payments made against the same mountain, and only one of them was
mandatory. That is what it costs to keep time. The bill, I think, arrives for the
difficulty, never the hours.
