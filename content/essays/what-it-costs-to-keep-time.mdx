A rider crests a climb mid-Tour, hand loose on the tops, and on his wrist is a Richard
Mille worth more than the team car idling at the foot of the mountain. The watch is doing
nothing. It is not timing the stage — the bike computer does that, and does it better. It
is there to survive the cobbles, which is the only thing the moment asks of it that it is
genuinely good at.

A few hundred miles away, a marathon is won in a plastic GPS watch that costs less than
the winner's shoes. That one is doing everything: pace, lap, heart rate, the splits that
decide whether the record falls. It is the most useful object in the race. Nobody will
photograph it.

This is not a contradiction to resolve. It is the subject.

## Accuracy stopped being the question

For most of the wristwatch's life, the expensive watch and the accurate watch were the
same object. You paid more and you got closer to true. That world is gone. A cheap quartz
movement now keeps time better than the finest mechanical one ever made, and a GPS watch
that disciplines itself against an atomic clock in orbit is closer to true than anything
on any collector's wrist. The plastic thing on the marathon winner is, by every measure
that the word "accuracy" actually means, the better instrument.

So the buyer of the £300,000 watch is not buying accuracy. He cannot be. The market has
made accuracy almost free, and handed it to the cheapest object in the room. Whatever the
price is buying, it is something else — and the whole question of value in watches starts
the moment you accept that the obvious answer is wrong.

## Two kinds of keeping time

The Coros keeps time the way a tool keeps time: by counting, electrically, against a
reference it trusts completely. It is right to within a rounding error and it does not
care that you admire it. Switch it off and nothing is lost but the battery's patience.

The Richard Mille keeps time the way a person keeps a promise: imperfectly, through a
hundred small parts that have to agree, regulated by an escapement a human being adjusted
and finished by hand. It is *less* accurate than the plastic one. That is not the flaw in
it. That is the whole of it. You are not paying for the time. You are paying for the
difficulty of having coaxed a verdict on the time out of metal at all — for the fact that
someone solved, mechanically, by feel, a problem a battery now solves for nothing.

That is the line between the two reserves on the mountain. The rider has a finite reserve
of glycogen and will, and he is spending it. The mainspring has a finite reserve too,
wound that morning, unwinding all day against the same hill. One of those climbs is
strictly necessary. The other is a man and a brand agreeing to carry an unnecessary
difficulty up a mountain because the difficulty is the point. The unnecessary one is the
interesting one. It always is.

## The athlete already understands this

Ask a cyclist why he rides a steel frame he built up by feel when a faster carbon one sits
in the same shop, and he will hand you the watch collector's argument back, word for word,
in different clothes. Efficiency is not the only value. There is a value in the made thing,
in the visible evidence of a hand, in choosing the harder version on purpose because the
harder version is the one that means something. He climbed the mountain when the tunnel was
right there. He is not going to be confused by a watch that does the same.

This is why the serious athlete is the one audience on earth that needs no convincing on
the subject. The rest of us are taught to ask what a thing is *for*, and to suspect any
answer that isn't use. The athlete has already spent a career proving that the useful path
and the meaningful path are different roads, and that the second one is worth more — paid
for in a currency, suffering, that does not show up on any spec sheet. A watch that costs
more than it can ever earn back in function is not an affront to that person. It is a
familiar idea in a smaller package.

## What the price is actually for

So when a six-figure watch turns up on a wrist that does not need it, the price is not an
embarrassment to wave away. It is the evidence. It is the measure of how much difficulty a
person was willing to buy when the easy version was free and lying right there on the next
wrist over.

Quartz won the argument about time. It won so completely that keeping time stopped being
worth paying for, and the whole market reorganised itself around the only thing left to
sell: the cost of having kept it the hard way. The plastic watch on the marathon winner is
the proof of how cheap accuracy got. The Richard Mille on the rider is the price of
refusing the discount. Two finite reserves, the same mountain, and only one of them had to
make the climb. That is what it costs to keep time. The bill arrives for the difficulty,
never the hours.
