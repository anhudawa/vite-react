You already own the instrument that measures your training. It lives on the other wrist, charges by cable, and knows your threshold pace better than you do. The automatic is a different purchase. It measures nothing about your body; it runs on your motion and asks only that you keep moving, which is a promise you have already made. Buying the first one well comes down to five things that carry the whole decision — and two that carry almost none.

## Start with a boring movement

The right first movement is a common one. A handful of automatic calibers — Seiko's NH series, Miyota's 9 series, Sellita's SW200 family — sit inside thousands of watches from hundreds of brands, and that ubiquity is the point. Parts are everywhere. Any competent watchmaker has had one on the bench a hundred times, so a problem in year six is a routine repair instead of a quest. Expect drift of seconds a day — on the cheapest calibers, sometimes tens of them — and let it go; that is the nature of the machine. Exotic in-house calibers have their pleasures, but they belong to the second or third watch, once you know what you want from the hobby.

## Fit is a lug-to-lug problem

Endurance sport leaves you with less wrist than most buyers bring to a boutique — more tendon than tissue, and nowhere for a big case to hide. The spec everyone quotes is case diameter. The spec that decides how a watch wears is [lug-to-lug](/guides/sizing-a-watch-for-a-lean-wrist): the full span from the tip of one lug to the tip of the other. If that span reaches past the flat of your wrist, the watch will perch and rock no matter how modest the diameter sounds.

<Pull cite="FIT">The case does not have to impress the room. It has to clear the flat of your wrist.</Pull>

Measure that flat with a ruler before you shop, and treat it as your ceiling. Try watches on when you can. When you can't, cut a strip of card to the lug-to-lug length and lay it across your wrist. Thirty seconds of cardboard has saved a lot of returns.

## Buy margin, not a rating

Water-resistance numbers are set under still, ideal laboratory conditions. A flip turn is neither. The working consensus treats [100 meters as the sensible floor](/guides/water-resistance-for-swimmers) for a watch you will actually swim in, and a screw-down crown — one that threads shut against the case to seal it — as cheap insurance for pool and open water alike. The habit that matters costs nothing: check the crown is seated before you get wet. And remember the seals are rubber. Rubber ages. Chlorine, salt, and sunscreen all take their share, so rinse the watch in fresh water after a swim and have the resistance pressure-tested every couple of years.

## The strap does the working hours

Sweat ruins leather; save it for dinner. For the hours around training, [rubber and woven nylon](/guides/straps-for-sport) dry fast and rinse clean, and a bracelet handles sweat well if you flush the links now and then. Look for a standard lug width, which opens the whole aftermarket, and quick-release spring bars, which turn a strap change from a toolkit job into a ten-second one. One watch, two straps, and you have covered the pool deck and the wedding.

## Plan the decade, not the checkout

An automatic is bought once and kept up forever, so price the keeping. A full service — cleaning, fresh oils, new gaskets, regulation — comes due somewhere between five years and ten, depending on the brand and how hard the watch lives; watchmakers who deal with pool water put five at the outside, not the inside. A common movement keeps that bill sane; for the most ubiquitous calibers, replacing the movement outright can cost less than overhauling it, so ask for both quotes. The modern hazard worth knowing is magnetism. Phone cases, speaker grilles, and bag clasps can magnetize a hairspring and throw the rate hard in either direction. The cure is a demagnetizer and a moment of a watchmaker's time, but it is easier to keep the watch off the magnetic charging pad in the first place.

## What you can ignore

Two things, mostly. First, complications. A date window earns its place; a mechanical chronograph you will never start does not, because your GPS watch already owns the data and every added function is more parts to fail and more to pay at service. Second, prestige. The name on the dial changes what strangers think of the watch, and very little about how it runs, fits, seals, or gets serviced. The premium is real; what it buys is not on your wrist.

Get those five right and the seventh — the wanting — takes care of itself. The first automatic should be the one you can wear through a swim, a long ride, and a service interval without flinching. Everything else is the second watch's problem.
